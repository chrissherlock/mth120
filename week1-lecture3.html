1. Sequences and Progressions

📖 Notation Reference: Sequence Mechanics

Think of a sequence simply as an endless ordered list —like a musical playlist or numbered parking spots—where every step has its own designated number.
$(a_n)$Sequence $(a_0, a_1, a_2, \dots)$
$an + b$Arithmetic progression
$aq^n$Geometric progression

To truly grasp what a sequence is, look at it through two complementary lenses:

  • 1. The List View (Intuitive): An endless, ordered string of numbers $(a_n) = (a_1, a_2, a_3, a_4, \dots)$. Order matters deeply: $(1, 2, 3, \dots)$ is completely different from $(3, 2, 1, \dots)$.
  • 2. The Function View (Rigorous): Formally, a sequence is a function whose domain is the natural numbers $\mathbb{N}$ and whose codomain is the real numbers $\mathbb{R}$. Instead of $f(n)$, we write $a_n$:
    • Input ($n$): The position or index ($0, 1, 2, 3, \dots$).
    • Output ($a_n$): The actual real number sitting at that position.

💡 Recipe Analogy: Explicit vs. Recursive Formulas

  • Explicit Formula (Instant Recipe): Tells you how to compute the 100th term right now without computing the first 99 (e.g., $a_n = 3n + 2$).
  • Recursive Formula (Step-by-Step Recipe): Tells you, "Take yesterday's term and add two extra to it." You must know the previous term to find the next (e.g., $a_1 = 5, a_n = a_{n-1} + 2$).

2. Finite Sample vs. The Infinite Sequence

VISUALIZATION: Finite Sample vs. The Infinite Sequence

The Infinite Reality: Any computational plot only ever reveals a finite sample (the blue terms $a_1, \dots, a_6$). The emerald green dots represent the infinite tail ($a_7, a_8, a_9, \dots$) which continues without end for every $n \in \mathbb{N}$.

3. Sums and Partial Sums

📖 Notation Reference: Summation Mechanics

Meet the sigma machine: The Greek letter sigma ($\sum$) is shorthand for adding up a string of numbers without having to write out endless plus signs.
$\sum$Sigma operator: instruction to sum terms together
$\nu = 0$ or $1$Lower limit: starting index value
$n$Upper limit: final index value where the sum stops
$s_n$Partial sum: the running total score up to index $n$

Writing out $b_0 + b_1 + \dots + b_n$ becomes messy, so we define partial sums ($s_n$):

$$s_n = \sum_{\nu=0}^{n} b_\nu = b_0 + b_1 + b_2 + \dots + b_n$$

Two essential summation formulas appear repeatedly:

  • Triangular Numbers: $\sum_{\nu=1}^{n} \nu = 1 + 2 + \dots + n = \frac{n(n+1)}{2}$
  • Geometric Series: $\sum_{\nu=0}^{n-1} q^\nu = \frac{1 - q^n}{1 - q} \quad (q \neq 1)$

🎯 Worked Example: Telescoping Cancellations

Evaluate $\sum_{k=1}^{n} \left(\frac{1}{k} - \frac{1}{k+1}\right)$ by expanding terms:

$$\left(1 - \frac{1}{2}\right) + \left(\frac{1}{2} - \frac{1}{3}\right) + \left(\frac{1}{3} - \frac{1}{4}\right) + \dots + \left(\frac{1}{n} - \frac{1}{n+1}\right)$$

All interior terms cancel out, leaving only the first and last: $1 - \frac{1}{n+1} = \frac{n}{n+1}$.

4. Derived Sequences (The Speedometer)

⚖️ Side-by-Side: Derived Sequences vs. Partial Sums

  • Derived Sequences ($a_n'$): Look locally at immediate neighbors to measure change / speed / slope ($a_{n+1} - a_n$).
  • Partial Sums ($s_n$): Look cumulatively backward at everything that came before to measure total accumulation / area ($\sum b_\nu$).

📖 Notation Reference: Derived Sequences

The Speedometer Analogy: While a sequence tells you your position (odometer), a derived sequence tells you how fast you are jumping from step to step (speedometer).
$a_n'$Derived sequence: $a_n' = a_{n+1} - a_n$

🎯 Worked Example: Squares and Differences

For $(a_n) = (1, 4, 9, 16, 25, \dots)$ where $a_n = n^2$:

  • $a_1' = 4 - 1 = 3$
  • $a_2' = 9 - 4 = 5$
  • $a_3' = 16 - 9 = 7$
  • $a_4' = 25 - 16 = 9$

The derived sequence is $(3, 5, 7, 9, \dots)$, with explicit formula $a_n' = 2n + 1$.

Proposition 4 (Monotonicity Tests via $a_n'$):
  • $(a_n)$ is constant $\iff a_n' = 0$ for all $n$.
  • $(a_n)$ is increasing (strictly increasing) $\iff a_n' \ge 0$ ($a_n' > 0$) for all $n$.
  • $(a_n)$ is decreasing (strictly decreasing) $\iff a_n' \le 0$ ($a_n' < 0$) for all $n$.

5. Telescoping Inversion and Gauss's Formula

Because derived sequences act as discrete differences, we invert them via telescoping summation:

$$\sum_{k=0}^{n-1} a_k' = (a_1 - a_0) + (a_2 - a_1) + \dots + (a_n - a_{n-1}) = a_n - a_0$$

Rearranging yields the original sequence reconstruction formula:

$$a_n = a_0 + \sum_{k=0}^{n-1} a_k'$$

🎯 Deriving Gauss's Formula via Difference Inversion

We wish to evaluate $\sum_{k=0}^{n-1} k$, finding a sequence $a_n$ whose derived difference is $a_n' = n$ with $a_0 = 0$.

Since $(n^2)' = 2n + 1$ and $(n)' = 1$, by linearity:

$$(n^2 - n)' = (2n + 1) - 1 = 2n \implies \left(\frac{n^2 - n}{2}\right)' = n$$

By telescoping inversion, $\sum_{k=0}^{n-1} k = \frac{n(n-1)}{2}$. Shifting indices $k \mapsto k+1$ gives Gauss's famous formula:

$$\sum_{k=1}^n k = \frac{n(n+1)}{2}$$
