Bridging the Gap: From Intuition to Epsilon-N Rigor

In introductory calculus, we are often told that a limit is a value that a sequence "approaches" or "gets closer and closer to" as $n$ marches toward infinity. While that intuition helps picture motion, pure mathematics demands precision: exactly how close is "close," and after what point is that proximity permanently guaranteed?

This week transitions from intuitive hand-waving to rigorous analytical thinking across four foundational themes:

  • The $\epsilon\text{-}N$ Definition: Turning limits into an exact challenge. You name any tiny tolerance $\epsilon > 0$, and the sequence produces a cutoff index $N$ beyond which every term stays trapped within that narrow window forever.
  • Limit Laws & Squeeze Theorem: Building an algebraic toolkit that allows us to evaluate composite limits without having to build an $\epsilon\text{-}N$ scratchpad proof from scratch each time.
  • Monotone Convergence & Recursion: Proving that a sequence must settle down simply because it is trapped and moving in a single direction—giving us the legal right to solve recursive limits algebraically.
  • Divergence to Infinity: Distinguishing chaotic oscillation from systematic, unbounded growth using the $M\text{-}N$ towering floor test.

Take your time with the scratchpad method. Working backwards to discover $N$ before writing out the formal proof forwards is a core skill in real analysis, and it becomes second nature with practice.

1. Formal $\epsilon\text{-}N$ Convergence

📖 Notation Reference: Sequences & Limits

Don't be intimidated by the symbols! If upside-down A's ($\forall$), backward E's ($\exists$), or little ceiling brackets ($\lceil \dots \rceil$) look unfamiliar, that is completely normal. They are simply mathematicians' shorthand for everyday concepts.
$\forall$Universal quantifier: "for all" or "for every"
$\exists$Existential quantifier: "there exists"
$(a_n)$Sequence: an ordered list $(a_1, a_2, a_3, \dots)$

$\epsilon > 0$Arbitrary positive distance tolerance

Before looking at the formal symbols, let's build the intuition: Saying a sequence converges to $L$ means that if you look far enough down the list, every subsequent term gets arbitrarily close to $L$ and stays there. We want a mathematical way to guarantee this "trapping" behavior.

A sequence $(a_n)$ converges to a limit $L$, written $\lim_{n\to\infty} a_n = L$, if:

$$\forall \epsilon > 0, \quad \exists N \in \mathbb{N} \quad \text{such that} \quad \forall n > N, \quad |a_n - L| < \epsilon$$

💡 Plain-English Breakdown: What is this formula actually saying?

If looking at $\forall \epsilon > 0, \ \exists N \in \mathbb{N} \ \text{such that} \ \forall n > N, \ |a_n - L| < \epsilon$ makes your head spin, think of it as an archery challenge or a game between two players:

  1. 1. The Challenger sets tolerance ($\epsilon$): Your opponent hands you a tiny positive distance $\epsilon$, drawing a narrow target band around $L$.
  2. 2. You find a cutoff step ($N$): You determine how far down the list to walk—past index $N$—so everything settles inside the band.
  3. 3. The Tail Test ($\vert{}a_n - L\vert{} < \epsilon$): The absolute distance between $a_n$ and $L$ stays smaller than $\epsilon$ for every step past $N$.

If you win no matter how small your opponent makes $\epsilon$, the sequence converges to $L$!

Mathematical Mechanics

Logical Rationale

💡 Quantifier Order, Scope, and Dependency

In formal analysis, statements often string together multiple quantifiers: $\forall \epsilon > 0 \quad \exists N \in \mathbb{N} \quad \forall n > N \dots$ To parse these without getting lost, think of them as nested scopes and sequential turns in a game.

⚙️ How Chained Quantifiers Actually Work
  1. Left-to-Right Evaluation Order: Quantifiers execute strictly from left to right like lines in a computer program. You cannot jump ahead to inner variables until all outer variables have already been instantiated.
  2. Lexical Scope & Function Dependency: Every quantifier opens a scope that wraps around everything to its right. Inner existential variables are allowed to "see" and depend on all previously declared variables:
    ∀ ε > 0 { ∃ N = N(ε) { ∀ n > N { |a_n - L| < ε } } }
    Because $N$ sits inside the scope of $\epsilon$, it is mathematically a function of $\epsilon$ ($N(\epsilon)$).
  3. The Commutativity Rule (Same vs. Alternating): Quantifiers of the same type commute freely without changing meaning ($\forall x \, \forall y \equiv \forall y \, \forall x$). However, alternating quantifiers ($\forall \exists$ vs. $\exists \forall$) cannot be swapped because swapping inverts who gets to react to whom.
VariableScopeDependency Rule
$\epsilon$$\forall \epsilon > 0$Outer scope. Chosen independently of everything that follows.
$N$$\exists N \in \mathbb{N}$Inner to $\epsilon$. Chosen after inspecting $\epsilon$ ($N = N(\epsilon)$).
$n$$\forall n > N$Inner to both $\epsilon$ and $N$. Runs over all indices strictly past cutoff $N$.
⚠️ The Concrete Consequence: The Fatal Quantifier Swap

Notice what happens to the dependency chain when you swap the first two quantifiers:

Correct Definition ($\forall \epsilon \; \exists N$):

"For every tolerance $\epsilon > 0$, there exists an index $N$..."
$N$ is allowed to react to $\epsilon$. Shrink $\epsilon \to 0$, and $N$ simply walks further down the sequence ($N = \lceil 1/\epsilon \rceil$).

Illegal Swap ($\exists N \; \forall \epsilon$):

"There exists a single index $N$ such that for every $\epsilon > 0$..."
Because $N$ is now declared outer to $\epsilon$, it cannot react to $\epsilon$. It must be chosen blindly upfront to satisfy all positive $\epsilon$ at once.

Why this destroys convergence: If $|a_n - L| < \epsilon$ holds for all $\epsilon > 0$ past some fixed $N$, the only non-negative distance strictly smaller than every positive number is zero ($|a_n - L| = 0$). That means $a_n = L$ for all $n > N$. The swapped condition is so restrictive that it only holds for sequences that become permanently constant. For typical convergent sequences like $a_n = \frac{1}{n}$, no single $N$ can work for all $\epsilon$, showing that nesting order dictates mathematical meaning.

🎯 Worked Example 1: The $\epsilon\text{-}N$ Scratchpad Method

Prove formally that $\lim_{n\to\infty} \frac{2n+1}{n} = 2$:

  1. Step 1: The Scratchpad Analysis. Start with $|a_n - L| < \epsilon$: $$\left|\frac{2n+1}{n} - 2\right| = \left|2 + \frac{1}{n} - 2\right| = \left|\frac{1}{n}\right| = \frac{1}{n} < \epsilon$$
  2. Step 2: Solving for $n$. Rearranging gives $n > \frac{1}{\epsilon}$.
  3. Step 3: Choosing $N$. Choose an integer $N \ge \frac{1}{\epsilon}$ (formally $N = \lceil 1/\epsilon \rceil$).
  4. Step 4: Formal Proof Write-Up. Given $\epsilon > 0$, let $N = \lceil 1/\epsilon \rceil$. For any $n > N$, we have $n > \frac{1}{\epsilon}$, which implies $\frac{1}{n} < \epsilon$, proving $\left|\frac{2n+1}{n} - 2\right| < \epsilon$. Q.E.D.

🎯 Worked Example 2: Applying Algebraic Limit Laws

Evaluate $\lim_{n\to\infty} \frac{3n^2 - 1}{2n^2 + 5}$ using the Limit Laws:

  1. Step 1: Divide by highest power. Divide both numerator and denominator by $n^2$: $$\lim_{n\to\infty} \frac{3 - \frac{1}{n^2}}{2 + \frac{5}{n^2}}$$
  2. Step 2: Apply Quotient & Sum Laws. Because $\lim \frac{1}{n^2} = 0$, we apply the arithmetic laws: $$\frac{\lim(3) - \lim\left(\frac{1}{n^2}\right)}{\lim(2) + \lim\left(\frac{5}{n^2}\right)}$$
  3. Step 3: Final Calculation. Substituting standard limits gives: $$\frac{3 - 0}{2 + 0} = \frac{3}{2}$$

2. Limit Theorems & Arithmetic

Computing limits directly from the formal $\epsilon\text{-}N$ definition for every new sequence can quickly become cumbersome. Instead, mathematicians build a toolkit of Limit Theorems—fundamental arithmetic rules and bounding principles that let us combine known limits to evaluate complex new ones instantly.

📐 Algebraic Limit Laws (Theorem 1)

Suppose $(a_n)$ and $(b_n)$ are convergent sequences such that $\lim_{n\to\infty} a_n = K$ and $\lim_{n\to\infty} b_n = L$, and let $c \in \mathbb{R}$ be a constant. Then:
1. Constant Rule: If $a_n = c$ for all $n$, then $\lim_{n\to\infty} c = c$.
2. Scalar Multiple: $\lim_{n\to\infty} (c \cdot a_n) = c \cdot K$
3. Sum / Difference: $\lim_{n\to\infty} (a_n \pm b_n) = K \pm L$
4. Product Rule: $\lim_{n\to\infty} (a_n \cdot b_n) = K \cdot L$
5. Quotient Rule: $\lim_{n\to\infty} \left(\frac{a_n}{b_n}\right) = \frac{K}{L}$, provided that $L \neq 0$ and $b_n \neq 0$ for all $n$.
6. Power Rule: $\lim_{n\to\infty} (a_n)^p = K^p$ (for real powers where terms remain defined).

💡 Intuition: Why Arithmetic Laws Work

These laws reflect a powerful principle: limits respect basic arithmetic operations. If sequence $A$ is zooming in on $K$ and sequence $B$ is zooming in on $L$, their sum, product, or ratio must naturally zoom in on $K+L$, $K \cdot L$, or $K/L$. The only exception is division by zero, which destroys the neighborhood structure around $L$.

The Squeeze Theorem (Sandwich Theorem)

Sometimes a sequence is too wild, complex, or oscillatory to evaluate directly using standard arithmetic laws. However, if we can bound it—trapping it from above by a larger sequence and from below by a smaller sequence that both converge to the exact same limit—the middle sequence has nowhere else to go. This powerful principle is formalized as the Squeeze Theorem (also known as the Sandwich Theorem):

Geometric Intuition: Imagine two tracker curves or enclosing walls closing in symmetrically from the ceiling and floor toward a common target $L$. As $n$ approaches infinity, the gap between the upper and lower bounds vanishes to zero. Any sequence forced to live inside that narrowing gap is mathematically compressed into sharing that exact same limit.

Let $(a_n)$, $(b_n)$, and $(c_n)$ be sequences such that $a_n \le b_n \le c_n$ for all $n \ge N_0$. If

$$\lim_{n\to\infty} a_n = L \quad \text{and} \quad \lim_{n\to\infty} c_n = L$$

then the middle sequence $(b_n)$ is forced to converge to the same limit:

$$\lim_{n\to\infty} b_n = L$$

🎯 Classic Example: Applying the Squeeze Theorem

Consider evaluating $\lim_{n\to\infty} \frac{\sin(n)}{n}$. Because sine oscillates between $-1$ and $1$, we know:

$$-\frac{1}{n} \le \frac{\sin(n)}{n} \le \frac{1}{n}$$

Since $\lim_{n\to\infty} \left(-\frac{1}{n}\right) = 0$ and $\lim_{n\to\infty} \left(\frac{1}{n}\right) = 0$, the Squeeze Theorem forces our middle sequence to also converge:

$$\lim_{n\to\infty} \frac{\sin(n)}{n} = 0$$

VISUALIZATION: Squeezing $\frac{\sin(n)}{n}$ between $-\frac{1}{n}$ and $\frac{1}{n}$ ($n = 1 \dots 30$)

Cautionary Contrast: What about the reciprocal $\frac{n}{\sin(n)}$?

It is easy to confuse $\frac{\sin(n)}{n}$ with its reciprocal $\frac{n}{\sin(n)}$. However, as $n$ grows while $\sin(n)$ periodically approaches $0$ near integer multiples of $\pi$, the ratio $\frac{n}{\sin(n)}$ shoots off toward $\pm\infty$ with wild, unbounded oscillations. Therefore, $\lim_{n\to\infty} \frac{n}{\sin(n)}$ diverges and the Squeeze Theorem cannot be applied here.

🎯 Worked Example: The Expanding Sum (Squeeze Theorem)

Evaluate the limit of the sequence $x_n = \frac{1}{n^2+1} + \frac{1}{n^2+2} + \dots + \frac{1}{n^2+n}$.

Note: We cannot use the standard Sum Limit Law here because the number of terms we are adding grows to infinity as $n$ increases. The law only applies to a fixed, finite number of terms. We must trap the entire sum.

  1. Step 1: Construct a Lower Bound.
    The sum has exactly $n$ fractions. The smallest fraction in the sum is the very last one (because it has the largest denominator: $n^2+n$). If we replace every single term in our sum with this smallest term, we get a sum that is strictly smaller: $$a_n = \underbrace{\frac{1}{n^2+n} + \dots + \frac{1}{n^2+n}}_{n \text{ times}} = n \left( \frac{1}{n^2+n} \right) = \frac{n}{n(n+1)} = \frac{1}{n+1}$$
  2. Step 2: Construct an Upper Bound.
    Similarly, the largest fraction is the first one (smallest denominator: $n^2+1$). If we replace every term with this largest term, we create a sum that is strictly larger: $$c_n = \underbrace{\frac{1}{n^2+1} + \dots + \frac{1}{n^2+1}}_{n \text{ times}} = n \left( \frac{1}{n^2+1} \right) = \frac{n}{n^2+1}$$
  3. Step 3: Establish the Squeeze.
    We have successfully trapped our expanding sum sequence: $$\frac{1}{n+1} \le x_n \le \frac{n}{n^2+1}$$
  4. Step 4: Take Limits of the Outer Bounds.
    Now, evaluate the limits of our new, simplified bounds: $$\lim_{n\to\infty} a_n = \lim_{n\to\infty} \frac{1}{n+1} = 0$$ $$\lim_{n\to\infty} c_n = \lim_{n\to\infty} \frac{n}{n^2+1} = \lim_{n\to\infty} \frac{1/n}{1 + 1/n^2} = \frac{0}{1+0} = 0$$ Because both the ceiling and the floor collapse to $0$, the Squeeze Theorem forces the trapped sequence to share the exact same limit. Thus, $\lim_{n\to\infty} x_n = 0$.

3. Limits and Supremum (Monotone Convergence)

While the formal $\epsilon\text{-}N$ definition lets us verify a limit when we already know it, how do we prove a limit exists for a sequence defined recursively or implicitly where the exact value is unknown? The answer lies in the profound link between sequence order and the Completeness Axiom of the real numbers.

Definition: Monotonicity

  • A sequence $(a_n)$ is non-decreasing (increasing) if $a_n \le a_{n+1}$ for all $n \ge 1$.
  • A sequence $(a_n)$ is non-increasing (decreasing) if $a_n \ge a_{n+1}$ for all $n \ge 1$.
  • A sequence is monotonic if it is either non-decreasing or non-increasing.

📈 Theorem 2: The Monotone Convergence Theorem (MCT)

One of the foundational pillars of real analysis: Every bounded monotonic sequence converges.
1. Non-Decreasing Case: If $(a_n)$ is non-decreasing and bounded above (i.e., $a_n \le M$ for some real number $M$), then $(a_n)$ converges, and:

$$\lim_{n\to\infty} a_n = \sup \{a_n : n \in \mathbb{N}\}$$

2. Non-Increasing Case: If $(a_n)$ is non-increasing and bounded below (i.e., $a_n \ge m$ for some real number $m$), then $(a_n)$ converges, and:

$$\lim_{n\to\infty} a_n = \inf \{a_n : n \in \mathbb{N}\}$$

💡 Why Does This Matter? (The Completeness of $\mathbb{R}$)

The Monotone Convergence Theorem is not true in the rational numbers ($\mathbb{Q}$). For example, consider the sequence of rational decimal approximations for $\sqrt{2}$ ($1, 1.4, 1.41, 1.414, \dots$). This sequence is non-decreasing and bounded above by $2$, but it does not converge within $\mathbb{Q}$ because its limit ($\sqrt{2}$) is irrational. The MCT relies entirely on the fact that $\mathbb{R}$ has no "gaps" (satisfying the Least Upper Bound Property).

🎯 Example: Evaluating a Bounded Monotone Limit

Consider the sequence $a_n = 1 - \frac{1}{n}$ for $n \ge 1$. Let's check its properties:

  1. Monotonicity: $a_{n+1} - a_n = \left(1 - \frac{1}{n+1}\right) - \left(1 - \frac{1}{n}\right) = \frac{1}{n} - \frac{1}{n+1} > 0$, so the sequence is strictly increasing.
  2. Boundedness: Every term satisfies $0 \le a_n < 1$, so it is bounded above by $M = 1$.
  3. Conclusion: By the Monotone Convergence Theorem, the limit exists and equals its supremum: $\lim_{n\to\infty} \left(1 - \frac{1}{n}\right) = \sup\left\{1 - \frac{1}{n}\right\} = 1$.

🎯 Worked Example: Applying the Monotone Convergence Theorem

Consider the sequence defined recursively by $x_1 = 1$ and $x_{n+1} = \frac{1}{3}x_n + 1$ for $n \ge 1$. Let's prove its convergence using the Monotone Convergence Theorem:

  1. Step 1: Prove Boundedness Above. We show by induction that $x_n < \frac{3}{2}$ for all $n$.
    Base Case ($n=1$): $x_1 = 1 < \frac{3}{2}$ (true).
    Inductive Step: Assume $x_k < \frac{3}{2}$. Then: $$x_{k+1} = \frac{1}{3}x_k + 1 < \frac{1}{3}\left(\frac{3}{2}\right) + 1 = \frac{1}{2} + 1 = \frac{3}{2}$$ Thus, the sequence is bounded above by $M = \frac{3}{2}$.
  2. Step 2: Prove Monotonicity (Increasing). We show that $x_{n+1} \ge x_n$. Let's examine $x_{n+1} - x_n$: $$x_{n+1} - x_n = \left(\frac{1}{3}x_n + 1\right) - x_n = 1 - \frac{2}{3}x_n = \frac{2}{3}\left(\frac{3}{2} - x_n\right)$$ Since we proved in Step 1 that $x_n < \frac{3}{2}$, the term $\left(\frac{3}{2} - x_n\right)$ is always positive. Therefore, $x_{n+1} - x_n > 0$, confirming the sequence is strictly increasing.
  3. Step 3: Invoke the MCT. Because $(x_n)$ is non-decreasing (increasing) and bounded above, the Monotone Convergence Theorem guarantees that $\lim_{n\to\infty} x_n = L$ exists as a finite real number.
  4. Step 4: Evaluate the Limit Algebraically. Now that existence is guaranteed, we take the limit on both sides of $x_{n+1} = \frac{1}{3}x_n + 1$: $$L = \frac{1}{3}L + 1 \implies \frac{2}{3}L = 1 \implies L = \frac{3}{2}$$ Thus, the exact limit is $L = \frac{3}{2}$ (which matches our least upper bound / supremum).

Evaluating Recursively Defined Sequences

When a sequence is given by a recurrence relation (e.g., $x_{n+1} = f(x_n)$) rather than an explicit formula, we cannot calculate its limit directly at first glance. But why can't we just take the limit algebraically right away? Understanding this reveals why a rigorous strategy is essential.

⚠️ The Core Dilemma: Explicit vs. Recurrence & The Trap of Blind Algebra

  • Explicit Formulas ($x_n = f(n)$): You have a direct formula where $n$ is isolated. You can take $\lim_{n\to\infty}$ instantly because you see the long-term behavior directly.
  • Recurrence Relations ($x_{n+1} = f(x_n)$): You are climbing a ladder one rung at a time. Each term depends entirely on the previous one, so $n$ does not appear as an independent variable.
  • The Logical Trap: If you try to take a shortcut by blindly assuming a limit $L$ exists and writing $L = f(L)$ (e.g., $L = \sqrt{2 + L}$), you are putting the cart before the horse. If a sequence diverges (like $x_{n+1} = x_n + 1$), a limit does not exist, and treating $L$ as a normal algebra variable leads to nonsense ($0 = 1$). Algebraic substitution assumes existence before proving it.

To make our algebraic calculation legally sound, we must split the problem into two distinct phases: proving the limit exists using the Monotone Convergence Theorem first, and calculating what it is second.

🛠️ The 4-Step Strategy for Recursive Sequences

  1. Step 1: Prove Boundedness. Use mathematical induction to show that all terms remain bounded above or below by a constant $M$. (This ensures the sequence doesn't run off to infinity).
  2. Step 2: Prove Monotonicity. Show that $x_{n+1} \ge x_n$ (increasing) or $x_{n+1} \le x_n$ (decreasing). (This ensures the sequence moves in a single direction without chaotic oscillation).
  3. Step 3: Invoke the MCT. Because the sequence is bounded and monotonic, the Monotone Convergence Theorem steps in as an absolute legal guarantee that $\lim_{n\to\infty} x_n = L$ exists.
  4. Step 4: Solve Algebraically. Now—and only now that existence is guaranteed—take the limit on both sides of the recurrence relation ($L = f(L)$) and solve for $L$.

🎯 Worked Example: The Nested Radical Sequence

Consider the recursively defined sequence given by $x_1 = \sqrt{2}$ and $x_{n+1} = \sqrt{2 + x_n}$ for $n \ge 1$. Let's evaluate its limit using our 4-step strategy:

  1. Step 1 (Boundedness): We show by induction that $x_n < 2$ for all $n$. Base case ($n=1$): $x_1 = \sqrt{2} < 2$ (true). Assuming $x_k < 2$, then $x_{k+1} = \sqrt{2 + x_k} < \sqrt{2 + 2} = 2$. Thus, bounded above by $2$.
  2. Step 2 (Monotonicity): We show $x_{n+1} > x_n$. Base case: $x_2 = \sqrt{2 + \sqrt{2}} > \sqrt{2} = x_1$. By induction, if $x_k > x_{k-1}$, then $\sqrt{2 + x_k} > \sqrt{2 + x_{k-1}}$, so the sequence is strictly increasing.
  3. Step 3 (Existence): By the Monotone Convergence Theorem, $\lim_{n\to\infty} x_n = L$ exists.
  4. Step 4 (Algebraic Evaluation): Taking the limit on both sides of $x_{n+1} = \sqrt{2 + x_n}$:

    $$L = \sqrt{2 + L} \implies L^2 = 2 + L \implies L^2 - L - 2 = 0$$

    Factoring yields $(L - 2)(L + 1) = 0$. Since all terms $x_n > 0$, the limit must be positive, giving $L = 2$.

4. Infinity as a Limit (Divergence to Infinity)

Not all divergent sequences bounce around chaotically like $\frac{n}{\sin(n)}$ or alternate forever like $(-1)^n$. Many sequences grow steadily larger and larger, marching off toward infinity. While these sequences do not converge to a finite number $L$ (and thus technically diverge), we assign a special classification: they diverge to infinity.

Formal Definition: Divergence to Infinity ($M\text{-}N$ Definition)

A sequence $(a_n)$ tends to infinity, written $\lim_{n\to\infty} a_n = \infty$, if:

$$\forall M > 0, \quad \exists N \in \mathbb{N} \quad \text{such that} \quad \forall n > N, \quad a_n > M$$

Similarly, a sequence tends to negative infinity ($\lim_{n\to\infty} a_n = -\infty$) if for every negative threshold $M < 0$, there exists an index $N$ such that $a_n < M$ for all $n > N$.

💡 Intuition: The Towering Floor Game ($M\text{-}N$ Game)

Compare this to our $\epsilon\text{-}N$ archery game for finite limits:

  • Finite Limit ($\lim a_n = L$): Your opponent gives you a tiny tolerance $\epsilon > 0$ to trap the sequence in a narrow neighborhood.
  • Infinite Limit ($\lim a_n = \infty$): Your opponent hands you an astronomically high threshold $M$ (e.g., $M = 1,000,000$).
  • Your Goal ($N$): You must find a cutoff index $N$ such that every term past $N$ towers above $M$ and never drops back down. If you can always find such an $N$ no matter how absurdly large your opponent makes $M$, the sequence diverges to infinity.

📖 How to Read This Diagram

  1. The Threshold Level ($M$): The dashed red line represents your opponent's arbitrary high demand $M$.
  2. The Cutoff Index ($N$): The orange dashed line marks the boundary $N$. Everything to the left is initial transient behavior; everything to the right is the long-term tail.

🎯 Worked Example: Proving $\lim_{n\to\infty} \sqrt{n} = \infty$

Let's use the formal $M\text{-}N$ definition to prove that the sequence $a_n = \sqrt{n}$ diverges to infinity:

  1. The Challenge: Given any arbitrary real number $M > 0$ (assume $M$ is large), we want to find an integer $N$ such that if $n > N$, then $\sqrt{n} > M$.
  2. Working Backwards: Solve the inequality $\sqrt{n} > M$ for $n$. Squaring both sides gives $n > M^2$.
  3. Choosing $N$: Choose any integer $N$ such that $N \ge M^2$ (or explicitly $N = \lceil M^2 \rceil$).
  4. Conclusion: For any $n > N$, we have $n > M^2$, which implies $\sqrt{n} > \sqrt{M^2} = M$. Thus, by definition, $\lim_{n\to\infty} \sqrt{n} = \infty$.
