Don't be intimidated by the symbols! If upside-down A's ($\forall$), backward E's ($\exists$), or little ceiling brackets ($\lceil \dots \rceil$) look unfamiliar, that is completely normal. They are simply mathematicians' shorthand for everyday concepts.

A sequence $(a_n)$ converges to a limit $L$, written $\lim_{n\to\infty} a_n = L$, if[cite: 24]:

$$\forall \epsilon > 0, \quad \exists N \in \mathbb{N} \quad \text{such that} \quad \forall n > N, \quad |a_n - L| < \epsilon$$

2. Limit Theorems & Arithmetic (§4)

[cite: 27]

Theorem 1 establishes that if $(a_n) \to K$ and $(b_n) \to L$, then sums ($K \pm L$), scalar multiples ($cK$), products ($KL$), and quotients ($K/L$ for $L \neq 0$) converge accordingly[cite: 27, 28]. The Squeeze Theorem (Theorem 4) proves that if $a_n \le b_n \le c_n$ and $\lim a_n = \lim c_n = L$, then $\lim b_n = L$[cite: 33].

3. Limits and Supremum (§5)

[cite: 31]

Theorem 2 establishes the Monotone Convergence Theorem: Any increasing sequence that is bounded above converges, and its limit equals its supremum ($\lim a_n = \sup\{a_n\}$)[cite: 31, 32]. This connects set completeness directly to analysis.

4. Infinity as a Limit (§6)

[cite: 37]

A sequence tends to infinity ($\lim a_n = \infty$) if for any large $M$, all terms past some index $N$ satisfy $a_n > M$[cite: 37]. Unbounded increasing sequences diverge to infinity (Proposition 7)[cite: 37, 38].
