Week 5, Lecture 15: Application: Optimisation

Welcome to Lecture 15: Finding the Best of All Possible Worlds

In the previous lectures, we learned how to mechanically compute derivatives and how to read them like a telemetry stream to map the shape of a curve. But calculus was not invented just to draw accurate graphs. It was invented to solve problems.

In the real world, resources are scarce. Engineers want to design a structural beam that maximizes load-bearing capacity while minimizing weight. Businesses want to set a price point that maximizes total profit. Epidemiologists want to find the exact day an infection rate will peak. All of these problems boil down to a single mathematical mandate: finding the highest or lowest possible value of a function. This process is called Optimisation[cite: 4].

🗺️ The Absolute Extrema Guarantee

Does a "best" or "worst" case scenario always mathematically exist? Not necessarily. If a function goes off to infinity, there is no maximum. However, we have a powerful safety net: Theorem 8 (Extreme Value Theorem).

If a function is continuous on a rigidly closed interval $[a, b]$, it is mathematically guaranteed to attain an absolute maximum and an absolute minimum somewhere on that interval. Our job is simply to hunt them down[cite: 4].

1. Local vs. Global Extrema

Before we start solving, we need to distinguish between two different types of peaks and valleys: Local and Global (also called relative and absolute).

Imagine hiking in a massive mountain range. You reach the top of a small hill. Relative to the immediate area around you, you are at the highest point—this is a Local Maximum. However, if you look across the horizon, there might be a massive, snow-capped summit that is the true highest point of the entire mountain range. That summit is the Global Maximum[cite: 4].

Relative (Local) Extrema

A point $c$ is a relative maximum if $f(c) \ge f(x)$ for all $x$ in some small, localized neighborhood $(a, b)$ directly surrounding $c$. It is a "turning point" in the middle of a curve[cite: 4].

Absolute (Global) Extrema

A point $x_{max}$ is the absolute maximum if $f(x_{max}) \ge f(x)$ for every single $x$ in the entire permitted domain. This is the ultimate peak of the entire system[cite: 4].

2. The Only Three Places to Look (Candidate Points)

If we are looking for the absolute highest or lowest value of a function on a closed interval $[a, b]$, we do not need to test every single decimal number. We only need to check three specific types of locations.

The Closed Interval Method

To find the absolute extrema of a continuous function on a closed interval $[a, b]$, you only ever need to evaluate the function at:

  1. Stationary Points: Where $f'(x) = 0$ (the curve smoothly flattens out into a peak or valley).
  2. Points of Non-Differentiability: Where $f'(x)$ is undefined (sharp, jagged corners or cusps).
  3. The Boundary Endpoints: The strict cutoff edges of the domain, $x = a$ and $x = b$. The absolute highest point of a mountain trail might simply be the edge of a cliff where the trail ends, even if it isn't a "peak"[cite: 4].

The river acts as a natural barrier, so you only need to fence three sides (two widths and one length). What dimensions should you choose to maximize the total enclosed area?

4. Warnings: When the Second Derivative Test Fails

In our paddock problem, the Second Derivative Test was incredibly fast because $A''(x) = -4$ was a constant. However, as noted in Theorem 20, the Second Derivative Test is not infallible[cite: 4].

⚠️ The Inconclusive Zero: $f(x) = x^4$

Consider the quartic function $f(x) = x^4$.

The first derivative is $f'(x) = 4x^3$. We find a stationary point at $x = 0$. Is it a maximum or minimum?

We try the Second Derivative Test: $f''(x) = 12x^2$. We evaluate this at our critical point: $f''(0) = 12(0)^2 = 0$.

The test is inconclusive!

Just because the second derivative is zero does not mean the point isn't an extremum. If we revert to the First Derivative Test, we see that $f'(-1) = -4$ (decreasing) and $f'(1) = +4$ (increasing). The curve clearly falls into a valley and rises, proving that $x=0$ is indeed a strict Relative Minimum despite the second derivative test failing[cite: 4].

Summary: The Peak of Differential Calculus

You have officially conquered the differential arc of this course. You started by hunting the ghost of division-by-zero, formalized the limit, and forged the algebraic rules of the derivative. Today, you wielded that machinery to solve practical, real-world constraints.

🎓 Key Takeaways from Lecture 15

  • Global vs Local: A local extremum is a peak relative to its immediate neighborhood. A global extremum is the absolute highest/lowest point across the entire domain.
  • The Search Grid: To find absolute extrema on a closed interval $[a, b]$, you only check stationary points ($f'(x)=0$), points of non-differentiability, and the boundary endpoints $a$ and $b$.
  • Verification is Mandatory: A stationary point is merely a candidate. You must mathematically verify its status using either the First Derivative Test (tracking slope changes) or the Second Derivative Test (evaluating concavity).
