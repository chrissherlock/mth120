Week 4, Lecture 10: Limits of Functions

Welcome to Lecture 10: Walking Up to the Edge of the Abyss

In Lecture 9, you learned how to prove that a curve is unbroken at a point $x_0$ using the $\epsilon\text{-}\delta$ game. That worked beautifully whenever $x_0$ was a safe, well-behaved input where the function value $f(x_0)$ actually existed.

But what happens when the point we care about most is a mathematical crime scene ? What if plugging in $x = c$ causes a division-by-zero explosion ($\frac{0}{0}$), leaving a microscopic hole punched right through our graph?

Today, we upgrade our $\epsilon\text{-}\delta$ microscope into a tool that can inspect the exact edge of a hole without ever falling in: The Limit of a Function .

🗺️ How This Fits Into The Grand Arc

All of differential calculus is built on a single paradox: to find the instantaneous speed of an object at a single moment in time, we have to divide an infinitely small distance ($\Delta y \to 0$) by an infinitely small time interval ($\Delta x \to 0$). Algebra says $\frac{0}{0}$ is illegal. Limits make it legal.

  • The Punctured Neighborhood: By adding one tiny constraint ($0 < |x - c| < \delta$) to the $\epsilon\text{-}\delta$ definition you already know from Lecture 9, we forbid $x$ from ever equaling $c$. We only care about the journey toward $c$, never the arrival.
  • Surgical Precision: Because $x \neq c$, we gain the legal right to factor, rationalize, and cancel out the exact $(x - c)$ terms causing the $\frac{0}{0}$ explosion—allowing us to calculate the exact height of a missing point.

1. The Punctured Neighborhood & Formal Definition

Let's set the scene with a classic mathematical trap. Consider the rational function $f(x) = \frac{x^2 - 4}{x - 2}$. On the surface, it looks like a standard, well-behaved algebraic curve. But if you attempt to plug in exactly $x = 2$, disaster strikes. The numerator evaluates to $0$, and the denominator evaluates to $0$, producing the dreaded undefined expression $\frac{0}{0}$. The machine completely seizes up. At exactly $x = 2$, the function has no output, no coordinate, and no physical reality. It is a microscopic hole in the fabric of the graph.

However, if we step just a fraction of a hair away from that danger zone, the machine behaves beautifully. If you plug in $x = 1.999$ (approaching from the left), the machine smoothly outputs $3.999$. If you plug in $x = 2.001$ (approaching from the right), it outputs $4.001$. It is incredibly obvious that both sides of this fractured curve are aiming directly at a target height of $L = 4$. The trajectory is undeniable. But how do we prove this rigorously when the actual point $f(2)$ does not exist to anchor our $\epsilon\text{-}\delta$ box?

This is where we must upgrade the $\epsilon\text{-}\delta$ game. In Lecture 9, our input fence was defined by $|x - c| < \delta$, meaning the distance between our test input $x$ and the target $c$ had to be less than delta. That old rule legally allowed $x$ to equal $c$ (a distance of exactly zero). To investigate limits safely, we must actively forbid the machine from ever touching the broken point. We do this by declaring that the distance between $x$ and $c$ must be strictly greater than zero.

Geometric Anatomy

The Punctured $\delta$-Window Ignores the Hole

Notice that at $x = c$, the curve has an open hole at height $L$, and the actual defined point $f(c)$ has been displaced upward. Because the limit uses a punctured neighborhood ($x \neq c$), the displaced point is completely ignored—the limit evaluates strictly to $L$.

Definition: The Limit of a Function ($\lim_{x \to c} f(x) = L$)

Let $f$ be defined on an open interval containing $c$, except possibly at $c$ itself. We say that the limit of $f(x)$ as $x$ approaches $c$ is $L$ , written $\lim_{x \to c} f(x) = L$, if for every positive number $\epsilon > 0$, there exists a positive number $\delta > 0$ such that for all $x$ in the domain:

$$\forall \epsilon > 0, \ \exists \delta > 0 \text{ such that } \mathbf{0 < |x - c| < \delta} \implies |f(x) - L| < \epsilon$$

Decoding the Formal Logic

If that wall of math symbols made your heart skip a beat, don't panic. You actually already know how to read this! It is the exact same Adversary vs. Defender game we played in Lecture 9, just with one critical modification to the rules. Let's translate it piece by piece:

  • The Output Challenge ($\forall \epsilon > 0$): The adversary still demands a strict output tolerance. They want the function to land within a microscopic distance ($\epsilon$) of the target height $L$.
  • The Input Response ($\exists \delta > 0$): You, the defender, still calculate a safe input fence ($\delta$) around the target input $c$.
  • The New Rule ($0 < |x - c|$): This is the massive game-changer. By demanding the distance between our test input $x$ and the target $c$ is strictly greater than zero, the mathematics physically prevents $x$ from ever touching $c$. You are safely orbiting the broken point, never falling in.
  • The Payoff ($|f(x) - L| < \epsilon$): If you follow the new rule and stay inside the fence, the machine outputs a number safely inside the adversary's $\epsilon$-corridor, proving the limit converges to $L$ regardless of whether the actual point $c$ is a mathematical crime scene.

Connecting the Shorthand to the Game

When you see the compact notation $\lim_{x \to c} f(x) = L$, it is easy to read it simply as "when $x$ gets close to $c$, $f(x)$ gets close to $L$." But mathematically, that shorthand is actually a compressed trophy of victory from the $\epsilon\text{-}\delta$ game.

Writing $\lim_{x \to c} f(x) = L$ is a binding declaration that the defender has a universal winning strategy. The $L$ in the equation is the center of the adversary's $\epsilon$-corridor (the target height), and the $c$ is the center of your $\delta$-window (the target input). Saying the limit equals $L$ means that no matter how aggressively the adversary shrinks the output error $\epsilon$ around $L$, you can always find a tight enough input window $\delta$ around $c$ (without ever actually touching $c$) to force the function safely inside the adversary's boundaries.

The Punctured Upgrade: Continuity vs. Limits

To understand why limits are the ultimate tool for handling mathematical holes, let's compare exactly how this new limit definition upgrades the continuity rules you learned in Lecture 9. The secret lies entirely in the left side of the implication (the input constraint). By adding a single zero to the inequality—demanding that $0 < |x - c|$—we fundamentally change the rules of the game.

Lecture 9: Continuity at $c$
$|x - c| < \delta \implies |f(x) - f(c)| < \epsilon$

Allows $x = c$ (distance $0$). Requires the actual point $f(c)$ to exist and serve as the target center of the $\epsilon$-corridor.

Lecture 10: Limit as $x \to c$
$0 < |x - c| < \delta \implies |f(x) - L| < \epsilon$

The clause $0 < |x - c|$ creates a punctured neighborhood. It strictly forbids $x = c$, testing only the surrounding points $(c - \delta, c) \cup (c, c + \delta)$.

3. One-Sided Limits & The Rendezvous Criterion

On a one-dimensional number line, there are two distinct directions from which you can walk toward a target point $x = c$: you can walk from the left (through numbers smaller than $c$), or you can walk from the right (through numbers larger than $c$).

Left-Hand Limit ($\lim_{x \to c^-} f(x) = L$)

The superscript minus sign ($c^-$) means we restrict our $\delta$-window strictly to inputs less than $c$:

$c - \delta < x < c \implies |f(x) - L| < \epsilon$

Right-Hand Limit ($\lim_{x \to c^+} f(x) = L$)

The superscript plus sign ($c^+$) means we restrict our $\delta$-window strictly to inputs greater than $c$:

$c < x < c + \delta \implies |f(x) - L| < \epsilon$

🤝 The Rendezvous Criterion (Existence of a Two-Sided Limit)

A two-sided limit $\lim_{x \to c} f(x) = L$ exists if and only if both one-sided limits exist and arrive at the exact same destination $L$:

$$\lim_{x \to c} f(x) = L \iff \lim_{x \to c^-} f(x) = \lim_{x \to c^+} f(x) = L$$

🌉 The Bridge Construction Analogy

Imagine two engineering crews building a bridge across a canyon toward $x = c$, one starting from the west bank ($x \to c^-$) and one from the east bank ($x \to c^+$). If both crews build toward the exact same altitude $L$, the bridge aligns—even if the final center bolt at $x = c$ hasn't been installed yet (a removable hole). But if the west crew builds at altitude $2$ and the east crew builds at altitude $5$ (a jump discontinuity), the bridge fails to meet, and the overall limit Does Not Exist (DNE).

4. Limit Laws & Algebraic Surgery ($\frac{0}{0}$)

Once we know that two individual limits $\lim_{x \to c} f(x) = L$ and $\lim_{x \to c} g(x) = M$ exist as finite real numbers, we do not need to play the $\epsilon\text{-}\delta$ game from scratch every time we combine them. The Algebraic Limit Laws guarantee that limits respect standard arithmetic:

⚙️ The Fundamental Limit Laws (Assuming $\lim_{x \to c} f(x) = L$ and $\lim_{x \to c} g(x) = M$)

  • Sum & Difference Law: $\lim_{x \to c} [f(x) \pm g(x)] = L \pm M$
  • Constant Multiple Law: $\lim_{x \to c} [k \cdot f(x)] = k \cdot L$ (for any constant $k \in \mathbb{R}$)
  • Product Law: $\lim_{x \to c} [f(x) \cdot g(x)] = L \cdot M$
  • Quotient Law: $\lim_{x \to c} \frac{f(x)}{g(x)} = \frac{L}{M}$, provided that the denominator limit is non-zero ($M \neq 0$).

Notice the crucial warning on the Quotient Law: it only works when the denominator limit $M \neq 0$. What happens when $M = 0$? There are two distinct possibilities:

  1. Case 1: Non-Zero over Zero ($\frac{L}{0}$ where $L \neq 0$): The fraction blows up toward $+\infty$ or $-\infty$ (a vertical asymptote, which we will master in Lecture 11). The finite limit Does Not Exist.
  2. Case 2: Zero over Zero ($\frac{0}{0}$ Indeterminate Form): Both numerator and denominator vanish at $x = c$. This signals a hidden common factor! We perform algebraic surgery to cancel the zero-producing factor before applying the Quotient Law.

🎯 Worked Example 1: Resolving $\frac{0}{0}$ by Factoring

Evaluate the limit: $$\lim_{x \to 3} \frac{x^2 - 2x - 3}{x - 3}$$

Step 1 (Check Direct Substitution): Plugging in $x = 3$ gives $\frac{3^2 - 2(3) - 3}{3 - 3} = \frac{9 - 6 - 3}{0} = \frac{0}{0}$. Because we get $\frac{0}{0}$, we know $(x - 3)$ must be a factor of the numerator.

Step 2 (Factor and Cancel): Factor the quadratic numerator $x^2 - 2x - 3 = (x - 3)(x + 1)$:

$$\lim_{x \to 3} \frac{(x - 3)(x + 1)}{x - 3}$$

Step 3 (Invoke the Punctured Neighborhood): Because $\lim_{x \to 3}$ examines only $0 < |x - 3| < \delta$, we are guaranteed that $x \neq 3$, so $x - 3 \neq 0$. We legally cancel $(x - 3)$ and evaluate the continuous twin function:

$$\lim_{x \to 3} (x + 1) = 3 + 1 = 4$$

🎯 Worked Example 2: Resolving $\frac{0}{0}$ by Radical Conjugation

Evaluate the limit: $$\lim_{x \to 0} \frac{\sqrt{x + 9} - 3}{x}$$

Step 1 (Check Direct Substitution): Plugging in $x = 0$ yields $\frac{\sqrt{9} - 3}{0} = \frac{0}{0}$.

Step 2 (Multiply by the Conjugate): Multiply numerator and denominator by the conjugate $\sqrt{x + 9} + 3$:

$$\lim_{x \to 0} \frac{(\sqrt{x + 9} - 3)(\sqrt{x + 9} + 3)}{x(\sqrt{x + 9} + 3)} = \lim_{x \to 0} \frac{(x + 9) - 9}{x(\sqrt{x + 9} + 3)} = \lim_{x \to 0} \frac{x}{x(\sqrt{x + 9} + 3)}$$

Step 3 (Cancel $x$ and Evaluate): Since $x \neq 0$, cancel $x$ from top and bottom, then substitute $x = 0$:

$$\lim_{x \to 0} \frac{1}{\sqrt{x + 9} + 3} = \frac{1}{\sqrt{9} + 3} = \frac{1}{6}$$

5. Trapping Wild Curves: The Squeeze Theorem

Sometimes a function is too wild for algebraic factoring, yet still converges to a single point. When a function $f(x)$ oscillates rapidly near $c$, we can prove its limit by trapping it between a floor function $g(x)$ and a ceiling function $h(x)$ that pinch together at the target point.

Theorem: The Squeeze (Sandwich) Theorem

Suppose that $g(x) \le f(x) \le h(x)$ for all $x$ in a punctured neighborhood around $c$. If the outer bounding functions converge to the exact same limit $L$ as $x \to c$:

$$\lim_{x \to c} g(x) = \lim_{x \to c} h(x) = L \implies \lim_{x \to c} f(x) = L$$
Visual Proof

Pinching an Infinite Oscillation: $-x^2 \le x^2 \cos(20/x) \le x^2$

As $x \to 0$, $\cos(20/x)$ oscillates between $-1$ and $+1$ infinitely many times. However, because its amplitude is multiplied by $x^2$, the entire wave is crushed between the upper parabola $h(x) = x^2$ and lower parabola $g(x) = -x^2$, forcing the limit at $x = 0$ to be $0$.

🌟 Landmark Geometric Squeeze: $\lim_{x \to 0} \frac{\sin(x)}{x} = 1$

By trapping a sector of the Unit Circle between an inner triangle and an outer tangent triangle (where $x$ is measured in radians), the Squeeze Theorem establishes the most famous limit in trigonometry: $$\cos(x) \le \frac{\sin(x)}{x} \le 1 \implies \lim_{x \to 0} \frac{\sin(x)}{x} = 1$$ This tells us that near $x = 0$, the sine wave $\sin(x)$ and the straight line $y = x$ become indistinguishable!

Summary: Inspecting the Unreachable

By introducing the punctured neighborhood ($0 < |x - c| < \delta$), we liberated our analysis from requiring $f(c)$ to exist. Whether a curve has a missing hole from a $\frac{0}{0}$ division or oscillates rapidly near a point, limits allow us to lock onto the exact destination of the trajectory.

🎓 Key Takeaways from Lecture 10

  • The Punctured Neighborhood ($0 < |x - c| < \delta$): A limit tests the behavior of $f(x)$ near $c$, strictly excluding $x = c$ itself.
  • The Rendezvous Criterion: The two-sided limit $\lim_{x \to c} f(x) = L$ exists if and only if the left-hand limit ($\lim_{x \to c^-} f(x)$) and right-hand limit ($\lim_{x \to c^+} f(x)$) both exist and equal $L$.
  • Algebraic Surgery on $\frac{0}{0}$: Because $x \neq c$, we can factor or rationalize expressions and legally cancel the zero-producing factor $(x - c)$ to evaluate the continuous twin function.
  • The Squeeze Theorem: When a function oscillates wildly near $c$, bounding it between two functions $g(x) \le f(x) \le h(x)$ that share the same limit $L$ forces $\lim_{x \to c} f(x) = L$.
