Week 3, Lecture 8: Transcendental Functions

Welcome to Lecture 8: Beyond Algebra

In Lecture 7, we built our foundation on algebraic functions: linear equations, power functions, polynomials, and rational expressions. These are the "building blocks" of early mathematics because they can be computed using a finite sequence of additions, subtractions, multiplications, divisions, and root extractions.

However, nature does not restrict itself to algebra. Sound waves oscillate periodically. Bacteria populations compound and multiply. To model these phenomena, we need a new class of functions that transcend standard polynomial algebra. These are known as Transcendental Functions .

Today, we will formalize exponential growth, demystify the natural logarithm, and establish the geometric and analytical rules governing trigonometric waveforms and their inverses.

1. Exponential Functions: From Discrete Steps to Continuous Growth

1.1 The Great Variable Swap: Power vs. Exponential Functions

In Lecture 7, we studied power functions like $f(x) = x^2$ or $f(x) = x^3$. Now, we perform a simple visual swap with massive mathematical consequences: we lock the base in place as a constant and move the variable $x$ up into the exponent.

Lecture 7 (Power Function)
$$f(x) = x^n$$
  • Base ($x$): Variable input.
  • Exponent ($n$): Fixed constant.
  • Growth Mechanism: Additive scaling. Increasing $x$ grows the base of a fixed-dimension geometric shape (like the side of a square $x^2$).
Lecture 8 (Exponential Function)
$$f(x) = a^x$$
  • Base ($a$): Fixed positive constant ($a > 0, a \neq 1$).
  • Exponent ($x$): Variable input!
  • Growth Mechanism: Multiplicative compounding. Every unit step forward in $x$ multiplies the current height by $a$.

To feel the difference in your bones, compare $x^2$ (power) against $2^x$ (exponential) as $x$ grows:

Input $x$ $1$ $2$ $3$ $4$ $5$ $10$ $20$
Power ($x^2$) $1$ $4$ $9$ $16$ $25$ $100$ $400$
Exponential ($2^x$) $2$ $4$ $8$ $16$ $32$ $1{,}024$ $1{,}048{,}576$

1.2 The Conceptual Puzzle: What Does $2^{\sqrt{2}}$ Actually Mean?

Here is the question that confuses almost everyone when they first study analysis: If an exponent tells us how many times to multiply a number by itself, how on earth can we raise $a$ to a fraction, a negative number, or an irrational number like $\pi$? You cannot multiply $2$ by itself $\sqrt{2}$ times!

In Week 1, we studied geometric sequences $a_n = a^n$, which only existed as isolated dots at integer steps $n = 1, 2, 3, \dots$. To turn those isolated stepping stones into a smooth, unbroken curve across the entire real line $\mathbb{R}$, mathematics builds a four-stage bridge , demanding at each stage that the fundamental rule $a^{x+y} = a^x \cdot a^y$ never breaks:

Stage 1: Natural Numbers ($x = n \in \mathbb{N}$) — Repeated Multiplication

This is our Week 1 geometric sequence. $a^1 = a$, $a^2 = a \cdot a$, and $a^n = \underbrace{a \cdot a \cdots a}_{n \text{ times}}$. Taking one step right ($n \to n+1$) multiplies the value by $a$.

Stage 2: Zero & Negative Integers ($x \in \mathbb{Z}$) — Walking Backward by Dividing

If stepping right ($+1$) multiplies by $a$, then stepping left ($-1$) must divide by $a$. Starting at $a^1 = a$ and stepping left to $x = 0$ gives $a^0 = \frac{a}{a} = 1$. Stepping left again to $x = -1$ gives $a^{-1} = \frac{1}{a}$, and $n$ steps left gives $a^{-n} = \frac{1}{a^n}$. Crucial takeaway: A negative exponent does not make the output negative—it simply flips the base into a fraction!

Stage 3: Rational Fractions ($x = \frac{p}{q} \in \mathbb{Q}$) — Splitting the Step into Roots

What is $a^{1/2}$? It is the halfway stepping stone between $x = 0$ and $x = 1$. If two half-steps make one full step, then multiplying $a^{1/2}$ by itself must equal a single full step of $a^1$: $$a^{1/2} \cdot a^{1/2} = a^{1/2 + 1/2} = a^1 = a \implies a^{1/2} = \sqrt{a}$$ In general, $a^{1/q} = \sqrt[q]{a}$ (the unique positive $q$-th root of $a$), and $a^{p/q} = (\sqrt[q]{a})^p$.

Stage 4: Irrational Numbers ($x \in \mathbb{R} \setminus \mathbb{Q}$) — Sealing the Gaps with Limits!

How do we compute $2^{\sqrt{2}}$? Here is where Weeks 1 & 2 pay off ! Even though $\sqrt{2} = 1.4142135\dots$ is irrational, we can trap it with a sequence of rational decimals: $q_1 = 1.4$, $q_2 = 1.41$, $q_3 = 1.414, \dots$. Since each $q_n$ is a fraction, $2^{q_n}$ is already defined by Stage 3! Because the sequence $2^{1.4}, 2^{1.41}, 2^{1.414}, \dots$ is monotonic and bounded, the Monotone Convergence Theorem guarantees it converges to a unique real limit, which we define as $2^{\sqrt{2}}$: $$a^x = \lim_{n \to \infty} a^{q_n} \quad \text{where } (q_n) \subset \mathbb{Q} \text{ and } q_n \to x$$

1.3 The Rules of the Base: Why Must $a > 0$ and $a \neq 1$?

Every textbook states that an exponential function $f(x) = a^x$ requires a base $a > 0$ with $a \neq 1$. These aren't arbitrary rules made up to annoy you—if you violate them, the continuous curve shatters:

  • Why $a < 0$ is forbidden (The Imaginary Swiss Cheese): Suppose you tried to use a negative base, like $f(x) = (-2)^x$. What happens at $x = \frac{1}{2}$? You get $(-2)^{1/2} = \sqrt{-2}$, which does not exist in the real numbers $\mathbb{R}$! The graph would be punctured with infinitely many holes at every fraction with an even denominator ($\frac{1}{2}, \frac{1}{4}, \frac{3}{4}, \dots$).
  • Why $a = 0$ is forbidden (Division by Zero): If $a = 0$, then for any negative input like $x = -2$, we would have $0^{-2} = \frac{1}{0^2} = \frac{1}{0}$, which is undefined.
  • Why $a = 1$ is excluded (The Flatline): Since $1^x = 1$ for every real number $x$, the function $f(x) = 1^x$ is just a horizontal constant line. Because it is completely flat, it fails the Horizontal Line Test (it is not injective) and has no inverse!

1.4 The Algebraic Engine: Translating Addition into Multiplication

The secret superpower of exponential functions is that they act as a bridge between two operations: they turn addition in the input (domain) into multiplication in the output (range) . For any $a, b > 0$ and any $x, y \in \mathbb{R}$:

Law Name Algebraic Identity What It Means Geometrically on the Graph
Zero Exponent $a^0 = 1$ Universal Anchor: Every pure exponential graph $y = a^x$ passes through the exact same $y$-intercept at $(0, 1)$.
Addition-to-Product $a^{x+y} = a^x \cdot a^y$ Shifting right by $+y$ units along the $x$-axis scales the vertical height of the curve by a factor of $a^y$.
Reciprocal / Mirror $a^{-x} = \frac{1}{a^x} = \left(\frac{1}{a}\right)^x$ Mirror Reflection: Replacing $x$ with $-x$ reflects the graph across the $y$-axis. Thus, $(1/2)^x$ is the exact mirror image of $2^x$!
Subtraction-to-Quotient $a^{x-y} = \frac{a^x}{a^y}$ Shifting left by $y$ units divides the vertical height by $a^y$.
Power of a Power $(a^x)^y = a^{xy}$ Changing the base from $a$ to $a^y$ is identical to horizontally compressing/stretching the input $x$ by $y$.

1.5 The Two Regimes: Exponential Growth ($a > 1$) vs. Decay ($0 < a < 1$)

Depending on whether your base $a$ is larger than $1$ or a fraction between $0$ and $1$, the exponential function splits into two mirror-image behaviors:

Regime 1: Exponential Growth ($a > 1$)

Example: $f(x) = 2^x$ — Strictly Increasing

  • Left Tail ($x \to -\infty$): Approaches the horizontal asymptote $y = 0$ (never touches or crosses $0$).
  • Right Tail ($x \to +\infty$): Explodes to $+\infty$.
  • Monotonicity: Strictly increasing ($x_1 < x_2 \implies a^{x_1} < a^{x_2}$).

Regime 2: Exponential Decay ($0 < a < 1$)

Example: $f(x) = \left(\frac{1}{2}\right)^x = 2^{-x}$ — Strictly Decreasing

  • Left Tail ($x \to -\infty$): Explodes to $+\infty$.
  • Right Tail ($x \to +\infty$): Decays toward the horizontal asymptote $y = 0$.
  • Monotonicity: Strictly decreasing ($x_1 < x_2 \implies a^{x_1} > a^{x_2}$).

🔑 Connecting to Lecture 7: Domain, Range, and Bijection

  • Natural Domain: $\mathbb{R} = (-\infty, \infty)$. You can plug any real number into an exponential function without fear of dividing by zero or taking even roots of negative numbers.
  • Range (Image): $(0, \infty) = \mathbb{R}^+$. Because the base $a > 0$, raising it to any real power always produces a strictly positive number ($a^x > 0$). An exponential function can never equal zero and can never be negative!
  • Injectivity & Invertibility: Because $f(x) = a^x$ is strictly monotonic (strictly increasing for $a > 1$, strictly decreasing for $0 < a < 1$), it passes the Horizontal Line Test everywhere. When we restrict its codomain to its range $(0, \infty)$, $f: \mathbb{R} \to (0, \infty)$ is a perfect bijection —which guarantees that its inverse function (the logarithm in Section 2!) exists!

1.6 Euler's Number $e$: Why Calculus Prefers $e^x$ Over $2^x$ or $10^x$

Out of all possible bases $a > 1$, there is one special irrational number that serves as the natural currency of calculus: Euler's number $e \approx 2.718281828\dots$ .

Where does $e$ come from? Recall from Week 2 (Lecture 5) that we proved the sequence $a_n = \left(1 + \frac{1}{n}\right)^n$ is strictly increasing and bounded above by $3$. By the Monotone Convergence Theorem, its limit defines $e$:

$$e = \lim_{n \to \infty} \left(1 + \frac{1}{n}\right)^n \approx 2.71828\dots$$

The Geometric Secret of $e^x$: Every exponential curve $y = a^x$ passes through the anchor point $(0, 1)$, but each base crosses that point at a different steepness (slope). If you graph $y = 2^x$, the slope of the curve as it crosses $(0, 1)$ is slightly flat (slope $\approx 0.693$). If you graph $y = 3^x$, the slope at $(0, 1)$ is slightly too steep (slope $\approx 1.099$). The base $e \approx 2.718$ is the exact "Goldilocks" base where the curve $y = e^x$ crosses $(0, 1)$ at a clean $45^\circ$ angle with a slope of exactly $1$! Even better, at every point on $y = e^x$, the slope of the curve is equal to its height $e^x$.

🎯 Worked Examples: Mastering Exponential Equations & Inequalities

Example 1 (Solving by Common Base Reduction): Solve $9^{x-1} = \frac{1}{27}$ for $x$.

  1. Spot the common prime base: Both $9$ and $27$ are powers of $3$ ($9 = 3^2$ and $27 = 3^3$).
  2. Rewrite both sides with base $3$: $$(3^2)^{x-1} = \frac{1}{3^3} \implies 3^{2(x-1)} = 3^{-3} \implies 3^{2x - 2} = 3^{-3}$$
  3. Use injectivity (one-to-one property): Because $f(t) = 3^t$ is strictly increasing, two outputs are equal if and only if their exponents are equal: $$2x - 2 = -3 \implies 2x = -1 \implies x = -\frac{1}{2}.$$

Example 2 (The Decay Inequality Trap): Solve $\left(\frac{1}{2}\right)^{x-3} > \frac{1}{8}$ for $x$.

  1. Express with the same base: Since $\frac{1}{8} = \left(\frac{1}{2}\right)^3$, the inequality becomes: $$\left(\frac{1}{2}\right)^{x-3} > \left(\frac{1}{2}\right)^3$$
  2. Watch the monotonicity direction! Because the base $a = \frac{1}{2}$ satisfies $0 < a < 1$, the function is strictly decreasing . Larger outputs come from smaller exponents! Therefore, stripping the base flips the inequality sign : $$x - 3 < 3 \implies x < 6.$$

2. Logarithmic Functions and Base $e$: Running Exponents in Reverse

2.1 Demystifying the Logarithm: The "Exponent Hunter"

For many students, the word $\log$ triggers an instant mental block. It looks like a strange computer command rather than a mathematical operation. To strip away the mystery, memorize this single sentence: a logarithm is just an exponent .

In Section 1, we saw that an exponential function $f(x) = a^x$ takes an exponent $x$ as its input and outputs a scaled number $y$. But what if we know the final scaled number $y$, and we need to hunt down the hidden exponent $x$ that produced it?

In Lecture 7, we proved that any strictly monotonic function is injective (one-to-one) and therefore possesses a unique inverse function when restricted to its range. Because $f: \mathbb{R} \to (0, \infty)$ defined by $f(x) = a^x$ ($a > 0, a \neq 1$) is strictly monotonic and surjective onto $(0, \infty)$, it is a perfect bijection . Its inverse function $f^{-1}: (0, \infty) \to \mathbb{R}$ is called the logarithm with base $a$ , written $\log_a(x)$.

🏛️ The Rosetta Stone of Logarithms (Definition by Equivalence)

For any base $a > 0$ ($a \neq 1$), any positive number $x \in (0, \infty)$, and any real number $y \in \mathbb{R}$:

$$y = \log_a(x) \iff a^y = x$$

How to read $\log_a(x)$ in plain English: Whenever you see $\log_a(x)$, translate it in your head as the question: "To what power must I raise the base $a$ in order to get $x$?"

  • $\log_2(8) = 3 \quad$ because $2^3 = 8$. ("What power of $2$ gives $8$? Answer: $3$.")
  • $\log_{10}\left(\frac{1}{100}\right) = -2 \quad$ because $10^{-2} = \frac{1}{100}$.
  • $\log_5(1) = 0 \quad$ because $5^0 = 1$.
  • $\log_9(3) = \frac{1}{2} \quad$ because $9^{1/2} = \sqrt{9} = 3$.

2.2 Mutual Annihilation & The Strict Domain Barrier ($x > 0$)

Because $f(x) = a^x$ and $f^{-1}(x) = \log_a(x)$ are exact inverse functions, applying one immediately after the other cancels both operations out, leaving your original input untouched. However, you must pay close attention to which inputs are legal in each direction:

Identity 1: Log of an Exponential
$$\log_a(a^x) = x \quad \forall x \in \mathbb{R}$$

"To what power must I raise $a$ to get $a^x$?" Obviously $x$! Because $a^x$ is always positive for any real $x$, this identity works for all real numbers $x \in \mathbb{R}$ .

Identity 2: Exponential of a Log
$$a^{\log_a(x)} = x \quad \forall x \in (0, \infty)$$

Raising $a$ to "the power that turns $a$ into $x$" produces $x$! Because $x$ is fed into $\log_a(x)$ first, this identity is strictly restricted to positive numbers $x > 0$ .

⚠️ The #1 Domain Trap in Calculus: Why $\log_a(0)$ and $\log_a(-5)$ Do Not Exist

In Lecture 7, we learned that when you invert a function, the Domain and Range swap places :

$$\text{Exponential: } \mathbb{R} \to (0, \infty) \quad \stackrel{\text{Invert}}{\Longleftrightarrow} \quad \text{Logarithm: } (0, \infty) \to \mathbb{R}$$

Because $a^y > 0$ for every real $y$, there is no exponent $y$ in the universe that can make $a^y = 0$ or $a^y = -5$. Therefore, you can NEVER feed zero or a negative number into a logarithm! Whenever you see $\log_a(\text{expression})$, your immediate reflex must be to set up the strict domain guard: $\text{expression} > 0$.

2.3 Geometric Mirroring: Reflecting Across $y = x$

As we established in Lecture 7, the graph of any inverse function $f^{-1}(x)$ is obtained by reflecting the original graph $f(x)$ across the diagonal mirror line $y = x$. Every coordinate pair $(u, v)$ on $y = a^x$ swaps to become $(v, u)$ on $y = \log_a(x)$:

  • The universal exponential $y$-intercept $(0, 1)$ flips to become the universal logarithmic $x$-intercept $(1, 0)$ .
  • The exponential base landmark $(1, a)$ flips to become $(a, 1)$ .
  • The exponential horizontal asymptote $y = 0$ (as $x \to -\infty$) flips to become a vertical asymptote $x = 0$ (the $y$-axis, where $\log_a(x) \to -\infty$ as $x \to 0^+$ for $a > 1$).

Base $a > 1$: Mirror Reflection

$y = 2^x$ and $y = \log_2(x)$ across $y = x$

  • Strictly Increasing: For $a > 1$, $x_1 < x_2 \implies \log_a(x_1) < \log_a(x_2)$.
  • Near Zero ($x \to 0^+$): Plunges to $-\infty$ along the vertical wall $x = 0$.
  • Right Tail ($x \to +\infty$): Grows to $+\infty$, but extremely slowly .

Base $0 < a < 1$: Strictly Decreasing

$y = \log_{1/2}(x) = -\log_2(x)$

  • Strictly Decreasing: For $0 < a < 1$, $x_1 < x_2 \implies \log_a(x_1) > \log_a(x_2)$.
  • Near Zero ($x \to 0^+$): Shoots up to $+\infty$ along $x = 0$.
  • Sign Rule: Positive for $0 < x < 1$, zero at $x = 1$, negative for $x > 1$.

2.4 The Algebraic Laws of Logarithms: Turning Multiplication into Addition

Why did Scottish mathematician John Napier spend twenty years inventing logarithms in 1614? Because multiplying and dividing huge numbers by hand is brutal, whereas adding and subtracting is easy! Since exponential functions turn addition into multiplication ($a^{u+v} = a^u \cdot a^v$), their inverse function—the logarithm—runs that machine backward: logarithms turn multiplication into addition, division into subtraction, and powers into scalar multiples!

Logarithm Law ($x, y > 0$) Formula One-Line Proof via Exponents ($u = \log_a x, v = \log_a y$)
1. Product-to-Sum $\log_a(xy) = \log_a(x) + \log_a(y)$ Since $x = a^u$ and $y = a^v$, $xy = a^u a^v = a^{u+v} \implies \log_a(xy) = u + v$.
2. Quotient-to-Difference $\log_a\left(\frac{x}{y}\right) = \log_a(x) - \log_a(y)$ Since $\frac{x}{y} = \frac{a^u}{a^v} = a^{u-v}$, taking $\log_a$ of both sides yields $u - v$.
3. Power Pull-Down $\log_a(x^p) = p \log_a(x) \quad (p \in \mathbb{R})$ Since $x^p = (a^u)^p = a^{pu}$, taking $\log_a$ of both sides yields $pu = p\log_a(x)$.
4. Reciprocal Flip $\log_a\left(\frac{1}{x}\right) = -\log_a(x)$ Special case of the Power Rule with $p = -1$: $\log_a(x^{-1}) = -\log_a(x)$.
🚫 Beware of Counterfeit Logarithm Laws!

Logarithms do not distribute over addition or subtraction, and a quotient of logs is not a difference! Never write these false equations in an exam:

$$\log_a(x + y) \neq \log_a(x) + \log_a(y) \qquad \text{and} \qquad \frac{\log_a(x)}{\log_a(y)} \neq \log_a(x - y)$$

2.5 The Natural Logarithm ($\ln x$) and the Change of Base Formula

Just as $e^x$ is the natural exponential function of calculus, its inverse function—the logarithm with base $e$—is called the natural logarithm and is given its own dedicated shorthand notation:

$$\ln(x) \equiv \log_e(x) \qquad \Big(\text{so } y = \ln(x) \iff e^y = x\Big)$$

Why does $\ln(x)$ rule calculus? Remember from Section 1.6 that $y = e^x$ crosses $(0, 1)$ with a clean slope of $1$. Because $y = \ln(x)$ is the mirror reflection of $y = e^x$ across $y = x$, $y = \ln(x)$ crosses its $x$-intercept $(1, 0)$ with a clean slope of $1$ ! In differential calculus, its derivative is simply $\frac{d}{dx}[\ln(x)] = \frac{1}{x}$, with no messy scaling constants attached.

The Change of Base Formula: Why You Only Ever Need One Logarithm

Here is a remarkable fact: every logarithm $\log_a(x)$, regardless of its base $a$, is just a vertically scaled copy of the natural logarithm $\ln(x)$!

Theorem (Change of Base Formula)

For any valid bases $a, b > 0$ ($a, b \neq 1$) and any $x > 0$:

$$\log_a(x) = \frac{\log_b(x)}{\log_b(a)} = \frac{\ln(x)}{\ln(a)}$$

3-Step Proof: 1. Let $y = \log_a(x)$. By the Rosetta Stone equivalence, this means $a^y = x$.
2. Take the natural logarithm $\ln(\cdot)$ of both sides: $\ln(a^y) = \ln(x)$.
3. Apply the Power Pull-Down Rule ($y \ln(a) = \ln(x)$) and divide by $\ln(a) \neq 0$ to get $y = \frac{\ln(x)}{\ln(a)}$. $\blacksquare$

This formula is also how we rewrite any exponential $a^x$ in terms of the natural base $e$: since $a = e^{\ln(a)}$, raising both sides to the power $x$ gives the universal calculus identity:

$$a^x = \left(e^{\ln a}\right)^x = e^{x \ln(a)}$$

🎯 Worked Examples: Logarithmic Domains, Equations & Inverses

Example 1 (Finding the Natural Domain of a Composite Logarithm): Find the natural domain of $f(x) = \ln(x^2 - 9)$.

  1. Set up the strict domain guard: The argument inside $\ln(\cdot)$ must be strictly positive: $$x^2 - 9 > 0 \implies (x - 3)(x + 3) > 0$$
  2. Analyze the sign of the parabola: The quadratic $x^2 - 9$ crosses zero at $x = -3$ and $x = 3$, and is positive outside its roots (where $x < -3$ or $x > 3$).
  3. Natural Domain: $(-\infty, -3) \cup (3, \infty)$. (Notice that $[-3, 3]$ is completely excluded!)

Example 2 (Solving a Logarithmic Equation & Checking for Extraneous Roots): Solve $\log_2(x) + \log_2(x - 2) = 3$.

  1. Establish the domain first! Both $\log_2(x)$ and $\log_2(x-2)$ must be valid, requiring $x > 0$ and $x - 2 > 0$. Thus, any valid solution must satisfy $x > 2$ .
  2. Consolidate using the Product-to-Sum Law: $$\log_2\big(x(x - 2)\big) = 3$$
  3. Unlock the logarithm using the Rosetta Stone ($y = \log_2(\dots) \iff 2^y = \dots$): $$x(x - 2) = 2^3 = 8 \implies x^2 - 2x - 8 = 0 \implies (x - 4)(x + 2) = 0$$
  4. Filter against the domain guard ($x > 2$): The candidate $x = -2$ fails because $\log_2(-2)$ is undefined! Only $x = 4$ survives.

Example 3 (Inverting a Shifted Exponential Function): Find the inverse $f^{-1}(x)$ of $f(x) = 3 \cdot e^{2x - 1} + 5$.

  1. Set $y = f(x)$ and isolate the exponential term $e^{2x-1}$: $$y = 3e^{2x - 1} + 5 \implies y - 5 = 3e^{2x - 1} \implies e^{2x - 1} = \frac{y - 5}{3}$$
  2. Apply $\ln(\cdot)$ to both sides to annihilate the base $e$: $$\ln\left(e^{2x - 1}\right) = \ln\left(\frac{y - 5}{3}\right) \implies 2x - 1 = \ln\left(\frac{y - 5}{3}\right)$$
  3. Solve for $x$ and swap variables: $$x = \frac{1}{2}\left(1 + \ln\left(\frac{y - 5}{3}\right)\right) \implies f^{-1}(x) = \frac{1}{2} + \frac{1}{2}\ln\left(\frac{x - 5}{3}\right), \quad \text{with domain } (5, \infty).$$

3. Trigonometric Functions: Sine and Cosine (From Zero to Calculus)

3.1 Why Do We Need Trigonometry in Calculus?

Look at every family of functions we have studied so far—linear, polynomial, rational, exponential, and logarithmic. As $x$ marches toward $+\infty$, every single one of them either explodes to infinity, plunges to negative infinity, or flattens out against a horizontal asymptote. None of them can repeat themselves.

Yet the physical universe is full of things that cycle over and over: pendulums swinging, alternating electrical currents, sound waves, ocean tides, and planets orbiting stars. To model repeating cycles mathematically, we need periodic functions . And the simplest repeating motion in geometry is walking around a circle .

3.2 First Principles: What is a Radian, and Why Does Calculus Ban Degrees?

If you have heard of angles in everyday life, you have probably heard of degrees ($360^\circ$ in a full circle, $90^\circ$ in a right corner). Where did the number $360$ come from? Ancient Babylonian astronomers chose $360$ because it divides cleanly by $2, 3, 4, 5, 6, 8, 9, 10, 12$, and is close to the $365$ days in a year.

In calculus, however, $360^\circ$ is a completely arbitrary human convention—it has no intrinsic connection to the size of a circle. If we want to do calculus on a function $f(x)$, the input $x$ and the output $f(x)$ must be measured in the exact same pure real-number units (lengths!). This is why calculus uses radians .

📏 The Physical Meaning of a Radian (The Circular Tape Measure)

Draw a circle of radius $r = 1$ centered at the origin $(0,0)$. We call this the Unit Circle ($x^2 + y^2 = 1$).

Now, stand at the rightmost point $(1, 0)$ and start walking along the curved rim of the circle. An angle of $x$ radians is literally the physical distance $x$ you have walked along the rim of the unit circle!

  • Positive angle ($x > 0$): Walk counter-clockwise (up and around) a distance of $x$.
  • Negative angle ($x < 0$): Walk clockwise (down and around) a distance of $|x|$.
  • Full Circle ($2\pi$ radians): The circumference of a circle is $C = 2\pi r$. Since our radius is $r = 1$, walking all the way around the unit circle once is a distance of $2\pi \approx 6.283$ units . Therefore, $360^\circ = 2\pi\text{ radians}$!
  • Half Circle ($\pi$ radians): Walking halfway around the circle to $(-1, 0)$ is a distance of $\pi \approx 3.14159$ units ($180^\circ = \pi\text{ radians}$).
Fraction of a Circle Zero $\frac{1}{12}$ Turn $\frac{1}{8}$ Turn $\frac{1}{6}$ Turn Quarter Turn Half Turn $\frac{3}{4}$ Turn Full Turn
Degrees ($^\circ$) $0^\circ$ $30^\circ$ $45^\circ$ $60^\circ$ $90^\circ$ $180^\circ$ $270^\circ$ $360^\circ$
Radians (Arc Length $x$) $0$ $\frac{\pi}{6}$ $\frac{\pi}{4}$ $\frac{\pi}{3}$ $\frac{\pi}{2}$ $\pi$ $\frac{3\pi}{2}$ $2\pi$

Conversion Formula: Since $\pi\text{ radians} = 180^\circ$, multiply degrees by $\frac{\pi}{180}$ to get radians, and multiply radians by $\frac{180}{\pi}$ to get degrees.

3.3 Defining Cosine and Sine: Your $(x, y)$ GPS Coordinates on the Unit Circle

Forget complicated triangle acronyms for a moment. Imagine riding in a glass cabin on a giant Ferris wheel of radius $1$ centered at $(0, 0)$. You board at the far right platform $(1, 0)$, and the wheel rotates counter-clockwise by an arc distance of $\theta$ radians.

Where is your cabin located now? Every point in the 2D plane has an horizontal coordinate and a vertical coordinate. Cosine and Sine are literally just the names we give to your horizontal and vertical coordinates on the unit circle!

Formal Unit Circle Definition

$$P(\theta) = \big(\cos(\theta), \, \sin(\theta)\big)$$

  • $\cos(\theta)$ (Cosine = Horizontal Shadow): Tells you how far left or right of the center you are.
  • $\sin(\theta)$ (Sine = Vertical Height): Tells you how far above or below the ground axis you are.
  • How does this connect to right-angled triangles (SOH-CAH-TOA)? Look at the right triangle inside the circle: its hypotenuse is the radius $r = 1$! Therefore: $$\cos(\theta) = \frac{\text{Adjacent}}{\text{Hypotenuse}} = \frac{\text{Adjacent}}{1} = \text{Adjacent}$$ $$\sin(\theta) = \frac{\text{Opposite}}{\text{Hypotenuse}} = \frac{\text{Opposite}}{1} = \text{Opposite}$$ The unit circle doesn't contradict right-triangle trigonometry—it simplifies it by setting the hypotenuse to $1$, while allowing $\theta$ to rotate through any angle in $(-\infty, \infty)$!

3.4 How to Find Exact Values Without a Calculator

In university mathematics exams, calculators are often banned or useless because you are expected to know exact values like $\cos(\pi) = -1$ or $\sin(\pi/6) = \frac{1}{2}$. You never need to memorize a giant table if you understand two simple geometric pictures:

1. The Extremes: Finding $0^\circ$ and $90^\circ$ (The Compass Points)

If you try to draw a right-angled triangle with an angle of $0^\circ$ or $90^\circ$, the triangle collapses into a flat line and standard trigonometry breaks down. But on the Unit Circle, these are the easiest points of all! Just picture where you are standing and read off your $(x, y) = (\cos \theta, \sin \theta)$ coordinates:

Starting Line: $0^\circ$ ($0$ radians)

You haven't moved. You are standing at the far-right edge of the circle (East). Your GPS coordinates are exactly $(1, 0)$ .

  • $\cos(0^\circ) = 1$: Your horizontal distance from the center is the full radius $1$.
  • $\sin(0^\circ) = 0$: You are flat on the ground axis; you have zero vertical height.
Top of the Wheel: $90^\circ$ ($\frac{\pi}{2}$ radians)

You walked a quarter-circle counter-clockwise to the very top (North). Your GPS coordinates are exactly $(0, 1)$ .

  • $\cos(90^\circ) = 0$: You are dead center horizontally; your shadow drops straight down to $0$ on the x-axis.
  • $\sin(90^\circ) = 1$: You are at the absolute maximum height of the Ferris wheel ($1$).

You can apply this exact same logic to the other two compass points:

  • At $180^\circ$ ($\pi$ radians, West): You are at $(-1, 0) \implies \cos(180^\circ) = -1, \quad \sin(180^\circ) = 0$.
  • At $270^\circ$ ($\frac{3\pi}{2}$ radians, South): You are at $(0, -1) \implies \cos(270^\circ) = 0, \quad \sin(270^\circ) = -1$.
Animated Visualization: The Collapse of SOH-CAH-TOA

Watch the horizontal shadow (Cosine) and vertical height (Sine) as the angle sweeps between $0^\circ$ and $90^\circ$. Notice how the triangle perfectly flattens out at the extremes!

2. The Three Famous First-Quadrant Angles ($\frac{\pi}{6}, \frac{\pi}{4}, \frac{\pi}{3}$) & The $\frac{\sqrt{n}}{2}$ Pattern

Where do strange numbers like $\frac{\sqrt{2}}{2}$ and $\frac{\sqrt{3}}{2}$ come from? Directly from Pythagoras' Theorem ($a^2 + b^2 = 1^2$):

  • At $\theta = \frac{\pi}{4}$ ($45^\circ$): The angle slices the first quadrant exactly in half, so the horizontal base and vertical height are equal ($x = y$). By Pythagoras: $x^2 + x^2 = 1^2 \implies 2x^2 = 1 \implies x = \frac{1}{\sqrt{2}} = \frac{\sqrt{2}}{2}$. Thus, $\cos\left(\frac{\pi}{4}\right) = \sin\left(\frac{\pi}{4}\right) = \frac{\sqrt{2}}{2}$.
  • At $\theta = \frac{\pi}{6}$ ($30^\circ$) and $\theta = \frac{\pi}{3}$ ($60^\circ$): Slicing an equilateral triangle of side length $1$ in half gives a short side of $\frac{1}{2}$ and a long side of $\sqrt{1^2 - (1/2)^2} = \frac{\sqrt{3}}{2}$.
🧠 The Unforgettable $\frac{\sqrt{0}}{2}, \frac{\sqrt{1}}{2}, \frac{\sqrt{2}}{2}, \frac{\sqrt{3}}{2}, \frac{\sqrt{4}}{2}$ Memory Pattern

To write down every special value in the first quadrant in 10 seconds, just count $0, 1, 2, 3, 4$ under a square root divided by $2$ for $\sin(\theta)$, and count backward $4, 3, 2, 1, 0$ for $\cos(\theta)$:

Angle $\theta$ $0$ ($0^\circ$) $\frac{\pi}{6}$ ($30^\circ$) $\frac{\pi}{4}$ ($45^\circ$) $\frac{\pi}{3}$ ($60^\circ$) $\frac{\pi}{2}$ ($90^\circ$)
$\sin(\theta)$ (Climbs $\uparrow$) $\frac{\sqrt{0}}{2} = \mathbf{0}$ $\frac{\sqrt{1}}{2} = \mathbf{\frac{1}{2}}$ $\mathbf{\frac{\sqrt{2}}{2}}$ $\mathbf{\frac{\sqrt{3}}{2}}$ $\frac{\sqrt{4}}{2} = \mathbf{1}$
$\cos(\theta)$ (Drops $\downarrow$) $\frac{\sqrt{4}}{2} = \mathbf{1}$ $\mathbf{\frac{\sqrt{3}}{2}}$ $\mathbf{\frac{\sqrt{2}}{2}}$ $\frac{\sqrt{1}}{2} = \mathbf{\frac{1}{2}}$ $\frac{\sqrt{0}}{2} = \mathbf{0}$

3. Beyond the First Quadrant: The ASTC Rule ("All Stations To Central")

The $\frac{\sqrt{n}}{2}$ trick gives you the exact numbers for the first quadrant. To find values in the other three quadrants, you just mirror the first-quadrant number and determine if it should be positive or negative.

Because $\cos(\theta)$ is your $x$-coordinate and $\sin(\theta)$ is your $y$-coordinate, their signs are dictated entirely by which quadrant you are standing in. This gives rise to the ASTC Rule (often remembered as "All Stations To Central" or "All Students Take Calculus" ), tracing counter-clockwise from Quadrant I:

Quadrant II
S
Sine is Positive

(Stations)
$x < 0$, $y > 0$
$\sin(+)$, $\cos(-)$, $\tan(-)$

Quadrant I
A
ALL are Positive

(All)
$x > 0$, $y > 0$
$\sin(+)$, $\cos(+)$, $\tan(+)$

Quadrant III

(To)
$x < 0$, $y < 0$
$\sin(-)$, $\cos(-)$, $\tan(+)$

Quadrant IV

Putting it into practice: If you need to find $\cos\left(\frac{4\pi}{3}\right)$:

  1. Identify the reference angle: $\frac{4\pi}{3}$ is just $\frac{\pi}{3}$ pushed into another quadrant. From your table, the value for $\cos\left(\frac{\pi}{3}\right)$ is $\frac{1}{2}$.
  2. Identify the quadrant: $\frac{4\pi}{3}$ is past $\pi$ (a half-circle) but before $\frac{3\pi}{2}$, landing it in Quadrant III .
  3. Apply ASTC: In Quadrant III ( T o), only Tangent is positive. Therefore, Cosine must be negative. Result: $\mathbf{-\frac{1}{2}}$.

3.6 The Five Structural Properties You Must Know for MTHS120

You do not need to memorize fifty obscure trigonometric formulas. In real analysis and calculus, almost every proof involving $\sin(x)$ and $\cos(x)$ relies on these five structural properties:

1. Natural Domain, Range, and Universal Boundedness

Because you can walk around the unit circle forever in either direction, the natural domain of both $\sin(x)$ and $\cos(x)$ is $\mathbb{R} = (-\infty, \infty)$ . Because no point on a circle of radius $1$ can ever stray further than $1$ unit from the center, their range is strictly $[-1, 1]$ : $$-1 \le \sin(x) \le 1 \quad \text{and} \quad -1 \le \cos(x) \le 1 \qquad \Big(\text{i.e., } |\sin x| \le 1 \text{ and } |\cos x| \le 1 \quad \forall x \in \mathbb{R}\Big)$$ Why this is huge for Calculus: Whenever you see $\sin(\text{anything})$ or $\cos(\text{anything})$ in a limit problem (like $\frac{\sin(n)}{n}$), you can immediately trap it between $-1$ and $+1$ and crush it with the Squeeze Theorem !

2. Periodicity (Period $T = 2\pi$) & Non-Injectivity

Adding $2\pi$ to an angle means walking one full lap around the circle and landing on the exact same spot. Therefore, for any integer $k \in \mathbb{Z}$: $$\sin(x + 2k\pi) = \sin(x) \qquad \text{and} \qquad \cos(x + 2k\pi) = \cos(x)$$ Because the waves repeat their values infinitely many times, $\sin(x)$ and $\cos(x)$ massively fail the Horizontal Line Test on $\mathbb{R}$ (they are not injective on $\mathbb{R}$, which we will fix by cutting their domains in Section 4!).

3. Symmetry: Cosine is EVEN, Sine is ODD

Imagine walking counter-clockwise by $+\theta$ versus walking clockwise by $-\theta$ from $(1, 0)$:
• Your horizontal shadow ($\cos$) is identical whether you step up or step down: $\mathbf{\cos(-x) = \cos(x)}$ ( Even function , symmetric across the $y$-axis).
• Your vertical height ($\sin$) flips sign (above vs. below the axis): $\mathbf{\sin(-x) = -\sin(x)}$ ( Odd function , symmetric around the origin).

4. The #1 Identity in Mathematics: The Pythagorean Identity

Look back at the right-angled triangle inside the unit circle with legs $\cos(x)$ and $\sin(x)$ and hypotenuse $1$. By Pythagoras' Theorem ($a^2 + b^2 = c^2$), for every real number $x \in \mathbb{R}$ : $$\sin^2(x) + \cos^2(x) = 1$$ (Notation note: $\sin^2(x)$ is shorthand for $(\sin x)^2$). Rearranging this lets you convert between sine and cosine at will: $\sin^2(x) = 1 - \cos^2(x)$ and $\cos^2(x) = 1 - \sin^2(x)$.

5. Phase Shifts & The Angle Addition / Double-Angle Formulas

Looking at the wave graph above, notice that the blue $\cos(x)$ curve is identical to the red $\sin(x)$ curve shifted left by $\frac{\pi}{2}$: $$\cos(x) = \sin\left(x + \frac{\pi}{2}\right) \qquad \text{and} \qquad \sin(x) = \cos\left(\frac{\pi}{2} - x\right)$$ When adding two angles $\alpha$ and $\beta$ (needed when we take derivatives of $\sin x$ and $\cos x$ later!), they expand via: $$\sin(\alpha \pm \beta) = \sin\alpha\cos\beta \pm \cos\alpha\sin\beta \qquad \text{and} \qquad \cos(\alpha \pm \beta) = \cos\alpha\cos\beta \mp \sin\alpha\sin\beta$$ Setting $\alpha = \beta = x$ gives the famous Double-Angle Formulas : $$\sin(2x) = 2\sin(x)\cos(x) \qquad \text{and} \qquad \cos(2x) = \cos^2(x) - \sin^2(x) = 2\cos^2(x) - 1 = 1 - 2\sin^2(x)$$

🎯 Worked Examples: Putting Sine and Cosine to Work

Example 1 (Using the Pythagorean Identity in Quadrant II): Suppose $\sin(\theta) = \frac{3}{5}$ and $\theta \in \left(\frac{\pi}{2}, \pi\right)$ (the second quadrant). Find the exact value of $\cos(\theta)$ and $\sin(2\theta)$.

  1. Use the Pythagorean Identity $\sin^2(\theta) + \cos^2(\theta) = 1$: $$\left(\frac{3}{5}\right)^2 + \cos^2(\theta) = 1 \implies \frac{9}{25} + \cos^2(\theta) = 1 \implies \cos^2(\theta) = \frac{16}{25} \implies \cos(\theta) = \pm \frac{4}{5}$$
  2. Determine the sign from the Unit Circle quadrant: Since $\theta \in \left(\frac{\pi}{2}, \pi\right)$, our point on the unit circle is in the top-left quadrant (left of the $y$-axis), where horizontal coordinates are negative! Thus: $$\cos(\theta) = -\frac{4}{5}$$
  3. Compute $\sin(2\theta)$ using the Double-Angle Formula: $$\sin(2\theta) = 2\sin(\theta)\cos(\theta) = 2\left(\frac{3}{5}\right)\left(-\frac{4}{5}\right) = -\frac{24}{25}.$$

Example 2 (Finding All Roots of a Trigonometric Equation): Find all solutions $x \in [0, 2\pi]$ to the equation $2\sin(x) - 1 = 0$.

  1. Isolate $\sin(x)$: $$2\sin(x) - 1 = 0 \implies \sin(x) = \frac{1}{2}$$
  2. Translate to the Unit Circle: Remember that $\sin(x)$ is the vertical height on the unit circle. We are asking: "At what angles on the unit circle is our vertical height equal to $+\frac{1}{2}$?"
  3. Find both intersections in $[0, 2\pi]$: From our first-quadrant table, height $+\frac{1}{2}$ occurs at $x_1 = \frac{\pi}{6}$ ($30^\circ$). But a horizontal line at height $y = \frac{1}{2}$ cuts the circle twice —once on the right side ($\frac{\pi}{6}$ above $0$) and once on the left side ($\frac{\pi}{6}$ before $\pi$): $$x_1 = \frac{\pi}{6} \qquad \text{and} \qquad x_2 = \pi - \frac{\pi}{6} = \frac{5\pi}{6}.$$

4. Tangent, Secant, and Inverse Trigonometric Functions: Slopes, Asymptotes & Inversion

4.1 Tangent and Reciprocal Functions: Slopes and Intercepts

Now that we understand sine and cosine as your $(x, y)$ position on the unit circle, what do the other trigonometric functions mean geometrically? They are simply built as ratios of sine and cosine:

Slope Definition
$$\tan(x) = \frac{\sin(x)}{\cos(x)}$$

Geometric Meaning: Tangent is the slope ($\frac{\text{rise}}{\text{run}} = \frac{\sin x}{\cos x}$) of the ray connecting the origin to your position on the unit circle. Unlike sine and cosine, tangent has a period of $\pi$ (not $2\pi$) because the ray points in opposite directions for angles differing by $\pi$.

Reciprocal Trio
$$\sec(x) = \frac{1}{\cos(x)}, \quad \csc(x) = \frac{1}{\sin(x)}, \quad \cot(x) = \frac{\cos(x)}{\sin(x)}$$

Geometric Meaning: Secant ($\sec$), cosecant ($\csc$), and cotangent ($\cot$) are the multiplicative inverses of cosine, sine, and tangent. Whenever cosine approaches zero, secant shoots off toward infinity.

Figure: The Tangent Graph $y = \tan(\theta)$ Across $[-2\pi, 2\pi]$ ($[-360^\circ, 360^\circ]$)

Therefore, the natural domain of $\tan(x)$ is all real numbers $\mathbb{R}$ excluding the odd multiples of $\frac{\pi}{2}$: $$\text{Domain}(\tan) = \mathbb{R} \setminus \left\{ \frac{\pi}{2} + k\pi : k \in \mathbb{Z} \right\}$$ Its range spans the entire real line: $\text{Range}(\tan) = (-\infty, \infty)$.

4.2 The Inverse Trig Paradox: How Do You Invert a Wave?

In Section 2, we found inverses by reflecting exponential curves. Can we do the same for sine? Let's try to find $\arcsin(1)$.

We are asking: "What angle has a sine (vertical height) of $1$?" Looking at the unit circle, you hit the top peak at $\frac{\pi}{2}$. But wait! If you keep walking around the circle, you hit top peaks again at $\frac{\pi}{2} + 2\pi$, $\frac{\pi}{2} + 4\pi$, or walking backward at $-\frac{3\pi}{2}$. There are infinitely many correct answers!

🚨 The Violation of Injectivity

Recall from Lecture 7 that a function must be injective (one-to-one) to have an inverse. Because periodic waves hit the exact same $y$-values over and over again, $\sin(x)$ fails the Horizontal Line Test catastrophically . If we tried to build an inverse without modification, one output point would map back to a messy, infinite set of angles—destroying the definition of a function!

The Analyst's Solution: Artificial Domain Restriction. To save the inverse, we surgically slice the infinite sine wave down to a single, unbroken monotonic hill that covers all possible $y$-outputs in $[-1, 1]$ without repeating any $x$-values.

Figure: Restrict the sine wave to one hill, then reflect it across y = x

4.3 The Three Core Inverse Trigonometric Functions

1. Arcsine ($y = \arcsin(x)$ or $y = \sin^{-1}(x)$)

  • Restriction Slice: We restrict $\sin(x)$ to the interval $\left[-\frac{\pi}{2}, \frac{\pi}{2}\right]$ (the right half of the unit circle, spanning from South to North). On this slice, sine climbs strictly from $-1$ to $+1$.
  • Domain (Input): $[-1, 1]$ (You cannot take the arcsine of $2$!).
  • Range (Output): $\left[-\frac{\pi}{2}, \frac{\pi}{2}\right]$ (Angles in Quadrants I and IV).
  • Rosetta Stone: $y = \arcsin(x) \iff \sin(y) = x$ where $-\frac{\pi}{2} \le y \le \frac{\pi}{2}$.
Figure: The Arcsine Graph $y = \arcsin(x)$ (Domain $[-1, 1]$, Range $\left[-\frac{\pi}{2}, \frac{\pi}{2}\right]$)

2. Arccosine ($y = \arccos(x)$ or $y = \cos^{-1}(x)$)

  • Restriction Slice: We restrict $\cos(x)$ to the interval $[0, \pi]$ (the top half of the unit circle, spanning from East to West). On this slice, cosine drops strictly from $+1$ down to $-1$.
  • Domain (Input): $[-1, 1]$.
  • Range (Output): $[0, \pi]$ (Angles in Quadrants I and II).
  • Rosetta Stone: $y = \arccos(x) \iff \cos(y) = x$ where $0 \le y \le \pi$.
Figure: The Arccosine Graph $y = \arccos(x)$ (Domain $[-1, 1]$, Range $[0, \pi]$)

3. Arctangent ($y = \arctan(x)$ or $y = \tan^{-1}(x)$)

  • Restriction Slice: We restrict $\tan(x)$ to the open interval $\left(-\frac{\pi}{2}, \frac{\pi}{2}\right)$, between its two central vertical asymptotes. On this slice, tangent sweeps continuously from $-\infty$ to $+\infty$.
  • Domain (Input): $\mathbb{R} = (-\infty, \infty)$ (You can take the arctangent of any real number!).
  • Range (Output): $\left(-\frac{\pi}{2}, \frac{\pi}{2}\right)$ (Strictly between the horizontal asymptotes $y = -\frac{\pi}{2}$ and $y = \frac{\pi}{2}$).
  • Rosetta Stone: $y = \arctan(x) \iff \tan(y) = x$ where $-\frac{\pi}{2} < y < \frac{\pi}{2}$.
Figure: The Arctangent Graph y = arctan(x) (Domain: ℝ, Range: (−π/2, π/2))

4.4 Mutual Annihilation: When Do Inverse Trig Functions Cancel?

Just as with logarithms, composing a trig function with its restricted inverse yields cancellation—provided the input stays within the legal domain/range bounds:

Trig of an Inverse (Always Works on Domain)
$$\sin(\arcsin x) = x \quad (\forall x \in [-1, 1])$$ $$\tan(\arctan x) = x \quad (\forall x \in \mathbb{R})$$

Because the inner function ($\arcsin x$) restricts its output strictly to the valid domain branch, feeding it into $\sin(\cdot)$ always annihilates cleanly.

⚠️ Inverse of a Trig (The Range Trap!)
$$\arcsin(\sin x) = x \quad \text{ONLY IF } x \in \left[-\frac{\pi}{2}, \frac{\pi}{2}\right]$$

Classic Exam Trap: What is $\arcsin(\sin(\pi))$? A careless student writes $\pi$. But $\pi \approx 3.14$, which is outside the range $\left[-\frac{\pi}{2}, \frac{\pi}{2}\right]$! Correct answer: $\sin(\pi) = 0 \implies \arcsin(0) = \mathbf{0}$, not $\pi$.

🎯 Worked Examples: Evaluating Inverse Trig Expressions & Composite Functions

Example 1 (Evaluating Exact Inverse Trig Values): Find the exact value of $\arcsin\left(-\frac{\sqrt{3}}{2}\right)$ in radians.

  1. Translate to a sine question: Let $y = \arcsin\left(-\frac{\sqrt{3}}{2}\right)$. This means we want $\sin(y) = -\frac{\sqrt{3}}{2}$ subject to the range restriction $y \in \left[-\frac{\pi}{2}, \frac{\pi}{2}\right]$.
  2. Consult special angle values: We know from our first-quadrant table that $\sin\left(\frac{\pi}{3}\right) = \frac{\sqrt{3}}{2}$. Because our target is negative and our range is restricted to Quadrant IV (clockwise rotation), we take the negative angle: $$y = -\frac{\pi}{3}.$$

Example 2 (Composing Trigonometric and Inverse Functions): Evaluate $\cos\left(\arcsin\left(\frac{3}{5}\right)\right)$ without a calculator.

  1. Let $\theta = \arcsin\left(\frac{3}{5}\right)$ . By definition, $\sin(\theta) = \frac{3}{5}$, and $\theta$ lies in Quadrant I (since $\frac{3}{5} > 0$).
  2. We need to find $\cos(\theta)$ . Use the Pythagorean Identity $\sin^2(\theta) + \cos^2(\theta) = 1$: $$\left(\frac{3}{5}\right)^2 + \cos^2(\theta) = 1 \implies \frac{9}{25} + \cos^2(\theta) = 1 \implies \cos^2(\theta) = \frac{16}{25}$$
  3. Choose the correct sign: Since $\theta$ is in Quadrant I ($\left[0, \frac{\pi}{2}\right]$), cosine is positive: $$\cos(\theta) = \frac{4}{5}.$$

Summary: Transcending Algebraic Boundaries

We have officially moved beyond the finite operations of polynomials and rational curves. This lecture expanded our mathematical universe into transcendental functions—equations that describe continuous growth, decay, and periodic oscillation, requiring infinite processes to fully define.

🎓 Key Takeaways from Lecture 8

  • Exponentials and Logarithms: We established $e^x$ and $\ln(x)$ as the ultimate inverse pair, demonstrating how logarithms dismantle exponential growth and rely on strict domain boundaries ($x > 0$).
  • The Power of Restriction: Periodic functions like sine and cosine fail the Horizontal Line Test catastrophically. To invent their inverses, we had to artificially slice their domains down to single, strictly monotonic intervals (e.g., $\left[-\frac{\pi}{2}, \frac{\pi}{2}\right]$ for sine).
  • The Amnesia Trap (Mutual Annihilation): We uncovered the hidden dangers of evaluating inverse trigonometric composites. While $f(f^{-1}(x)) = x$ is safe, applying an inverse to a trig function—like $\arcsin(\sin(x))$—requires extreme caution because the inverse function will aggressively snap any starting angle back into its legal restricted zone.
