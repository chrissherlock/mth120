Week 5, Lecture 13: Derivatives of Elementary Functions

Welcome to Lecture 13: Building the Calculus Machine

In Week 4, we spent hours staring into the mathematical abyss. We used the $\epsilon\text{-}\delta$ limit definition to carefully calculate the slope of a tangent line from first principles. We proved that to find the instantaneous velocity at $x_0$, we had to set up a massive algebraic fraction, manually factor the numerator, cancel out the $\Delta x$, and then evaluate the limit.

If we had to do that grueling process every single time we encountered a new function in physics, economics, or engineering, science would grind to a halt. Today, we leave the manual labor behind. By applying limits to general algebraic structures just one time, we will forge a set of permanent "Lego brick" rules for differentiation. Once you master the Product, Quotient, and Chain rules, you will be able to instantly calculate the exact rate of change for nearly any function in the universe without ever touching an $\epsilon$ or $\delta$ again.

🗺️ The Concept of the Derivative Function

Previously, we calculated the derivative $f'(x_0)$ at one specific, fixed coordinate. Now, we expand that idea. If a function $f: X \to \mathbb{R}$ is differentiable at every point $x \in X$, we can create a brand new function $f': X \to \mathbb{R}$ that outputs the tangent slope at any input $x$.

This derivative function is commonly denoted as $f'(x)$ or using Leibniz's differential notation $\frac{df}{dx}$. If a function $y = f(t)$ models a process over time, physicists often denote its derivative (velocity) with a dot: $\dot{y}$ or $\dot{f}(t)$.

Forging the Base Parts: Powers, Sine, and Exponentials

Standard calculus often treats base derivatives as a list of algebraic flashcards to be memorized: "bring the power down," or "sine becomes cosine." However, if we treat calculus as blind symbol manipulation, the rules will collapse under the weight of working memory. By anchoring these abstract algebraic rules to physical geometry and tangible physics, we don't have to memorize the rules—we just remember the physical shapes.

The Power and Danger of the "Intuitive" Proof

The interactive stepper above demonstrates what mathematicians call an intuitive proof. For a beginner, this type of physical, spatial reasoning is absolutely vital. If you only learn calculus as a set of blind algebraic rules, your working memory will eventually collapse under the weight of abstract symbol manipulation. By seeing the physical square expand, you anchor the abstract rule ($2x$) to a tangible reality: the derivative of a square is simply the act of pushing out its two outer edges.

This physical approach is not a modern pedagogical trick; it is the exact conceptual framework that birthed calculus. In 300 BCE, Euclid mapped the static geometry of a cut square. By the 1630s, Pierre de Fermat "animated" Euclid's square to find the slopes of parabolas by introducing a tiny hypothetical nudge. In 1687, Isaac Newton formalized this in his Principia. Newton argued geometrically that the two outer rectangles represented the true flow of the area's growth, while the tiny corner square was infinitely smaller—a "moment of a moment"—and could simply be thrown away.

But the act of throwing away that corner square sparked one of the greatest controversies in the history of science. In 1734, the philosopher and cleric Bishop George Berkeley published a scathing critique of Newton's logic. Berkeley pointed out a glaring mathematical fallacy: if your tiny nudge ($\Delta x$) is a real, non-zero number, you cannot just delete the corner square because it is inconveniently small. But if $\Delta x$ is exactly zero, you cannot divide by it to find a rate of change. Berkeley famously mocked these vanishing increments, calling them "the ghosts of departed quantities."

This brings us full circle to the crisis of the Weierstrass Monster. Intuition is a powerful scaffold for learning, but human eyesight and physical analogies completely break down at the scale of infinity. If we rely only on intuitive geometry, we have no defense against logical fallacies or pathological curves. Bishop Berkeley’s criticism is exactly what forced later mathematicians to abandon physical shapes and invent the rigorous $\epsilon\text{-}\delta$ limit. The formal algebraic limit ($h \to 0$) is the rigorous engine that legally proves deleting that corner square is mathematically sound, protecting our calculations when our physical intuition ultimately fails.

From Static Stars to Dynamic Motion

Just as the expanding square protects students from treating the power rule as arbitrary symbol manipulation, the bouncing spring grounds the derivatives of trigonometric functions in physical reality. When beginners are told to simply memorize that the derivative of Sine is Cosine, it feels like a random algebraic switch. But by mapping it to kinematics—where the derivative of position is velocity—the quarter-phase shift becomes a physical inevitability. When a bouncing object is at its maximum height, it must momentarily stop moving. Therefore, when position (Sine) is at a peak, velocity (Cosine) must be zero.

Historically, Sine and Cosine were never meant to track motion. They were born in antiquity as static geometry. From the Greek astronomer Hipparchus mapping the heavens using the chords of circles, to the Indian mathematician Aryabhata formulating the modern sine ratio (jya) in the 5th century, trigonometry was used almost exclusively for static surveying, architecture, and tracking the fixed positions of stars. For over a thousand years, these functions had absolutely nothing to do with time, velocity, or rates of change.

The mathematical leap from static triangles to dynamic physics required two 17th-century breakthroughs. First, in 1660, English polymath Robert Hooke discovered the physical law of elasticity (Hooke's Law), proving that the force pulling a spring back to its center is directly proportional to how far it has been stretched. Shortly after, Isaac Newton invented calculus to measure continuous change over time. Yet, even Newton initially calculated the derivatives of sine and cosine using cumbersome infinite geometric series, rather than elegantly mapping them to physical oscillators.

The true unification happened in the 18th century, championed by the Swiss mathematical giant Leonhard Euler. Euler liberated Sine and Cosine from the static right-angled triangle and redefined them as continuous fluid functions of variables (like time). By applying calculus to Hooke's physical springs, Euler and his contemporaries proved that simple harmonic motion is mathematically governed by differential equations whose only solutions are Sine and Cosine. The interactive spring above is the culmination of Euler's work: proving that the derivative of a wave isn't just a geometric trick, but the fundamental law of momentum and energy conservation playing out in real time.

2. The Assembly Line: Product and Quotient Rules

We now have our base gears. But real-world models combine these functions through addition, multiplication, and division. How do we differentiate combinations?

⚙️ The Sum and Constant Rules

  • Sum & Difference: $(f \pm g)'(x) = f'(x) \pm g'(x)$
  • Constant Multiple: $(c \cdot f)'(x) = c \cdot f'(x)$

(The derivative of a sum is simply the sum of the derivatives. You can treat them as completely independent machines.)

However, the derivative of a product is NOT the product of the derivatives. If you have a growing population that is simultaneously increasing its consumption rate, the total consumption does not just scale simply—the two growing factors interact and magnify each other. We must use the Product Rule.

The Product Rule

Let $f$ and $g$ be functions that are differentiable at $x_0$. Then their product $fg$ is differentiable, and:

$$(fg)' = f'g + fg'$$

"Differentiate the first, leave the second alone, PLUS leave the first alone, differentiate the second."

The Quotient Rule

Let $f$ and $g$ be differentiable at $x_0$, and assume $g(x_0) \neq 0$. Then the quotient $\frac{f}{g}$ is differentiable, and:

$$\left(\frac{f}{g}\right)' = \frac{f'g - fg'}{g^2}$$

"Low D-High minus High D-Low, over the square of what's below."

3. Nested Gears: The Chain Rule

The most important rule in differential calculus is the Chain Rule. It allows us to differentiate composite functions—functions nested inside of other functions.

Think of an onion with layers, or a machine nested inside a larger machine. If an inner machine $g(x)$ spins twice as fast as the input $x$, and an outer machine $f(u)$ spins three times as fast as its input $u$, the total system spins $2 \times 3 = 6$ times as fast as the original input. You simply multiply the rates of change together.

The Chain Rule

Let $F(x) = f(g(x))$ be the composition of two functions. If $g$ is differentiable at $x_0$, and $f$ is differentiable at $y_0 = g(x_0)$, then $F(x)$ is differentiable at $x_0$, and:

$$F'(x) = f'(g(x)) \cdot g'(x)$$

"Take the derivative of the OUTSIDE (leaving the inside alone), then MULTIPLY by the derivative of the INSIDE."

4. Reversing the Engine: The Inverse Function Rule

What if we have a function $f(x)$ whose derivative we know, and we want to find the derivative of its inverse function $g(y) = f^{-1}(y)$? Because inverse functions are perfectly mirrored across the line $y = x$, their tangent slopes are perfectly inverted.

The Inverse Function Rule

If $f(x)$ is continuous and differentiable at $x_0$ with $f'(x_0) \neq 0$, and $g(y)$ is the inverse function of $f$ in a neighborhood of $y_0 = f(x_0)$, then $g(y)$ is differentiable at $y_0$ and:

$$g'(y_0) = \frac{1}{f'(x_0)}$$

🎯 Worked Example: The Natural Logarithm

Let $g(y) = \ln y$. We know this is the inverse of the exponential function $f(x) = e^x$.

We know that the derivative of the original function is $f'(x) = e^x$. According to our inverse rule:

$$g'(y) = \frac{1}{f'(x)} = \frac{1}{e^x}$$

But we need the answer in terms of the input $y$. Since $e^x = y$, we substitute $y$ back into the denominator:

$$\frac{d}{dy}(\ln y) = \frac{1}{y}$$
