Week 5, Lecture 13: Derivatives of Elementary Functions

Welcome to Lecture 13: Building the Calculus Machine

In Week 4, we spent hours staring into the mathematical abyss. We used the $\epsilon\text{-}\delta$ limit definition to carefully calculate the slope of a tangent line from first principles. We proved that to find the instantaneous velocity at $x_0$, we had to set up a massive algebraic fraction, manually factor the numerator, cancel out the $\Delta x$, and then evaluate the limit.

If we had to do that grueling process every single time we encountered a new function in physics, economics, or engineering, science would grind to a halt. Today, we leave the manual labor behind. By applying limits to general algebraic structures just one time, we will forge a set of permanent "Lego brick" rules for differentiation. Once you master the Product, Quotient, and Chain rules, you will be able to instantly calculate the exact rate of change for nearly any function in the universe without ever touching an $\epsilon$ or $\delta$ again[cite: 4].

🗺️ The Concept of the Derivative Function

Previously, we calculated the derivative $f'(x_0)$ at one specific, fixed coordinate. Now, we expand that idea. If a function $f: X \to \mathbb{R}$ is differentiable at every point $x \in X$, we can create a brand new function $f': X \to \mathbb{R}$ that outputs the tangent slope at any input $x$.

This derivative function is commonly denoted as $f'(x)$ or using Leibniz's differential notation $\frac{df}{dx}$. If a function $y = f(t)$ models a process over time, physicists often denote its derivative (velocity) with a dot: $\dot{y}$ or $\dot{f}(t)$[cite: 4].

1. Forging the Base Parts: Powers, Sine, and Exponentials

Before we can build complex machines, we need the basic parts. We will use the limit definition of the derivative one last time to forge the exact derivative rules for polynomials, trigonometric waves, and exponential growth[cite: 4].

The Power Rule

For any power $f(x) = x^p$, we apply the limit: $$f'(x) = \lim_{\Delta x \to 0} \frac{(x + \Delta x)^p - x^p}{\Delta x}$$ Using the binomial theorem, $(x + \Delta x)^p$ expands to $x^p + px^{p-1}\Delta x + o(\Delta x)$, where $o(\Delta x)$ contains all the higher powers of $\Delta x$ that crush to zero. The $x^p$ terms cancel, the $\Delta x$ divides out, and we are left with exactly:

$$f'(x) = px^{p-1}$$

Trigonometric Waves

For $f(x) = \sin x$, we use the addition formula $\sin(x + \Delta x) = \sin x \cos \Delta x + \cos x \sin \Delta x$. Using the fundamental trigonometric limits $\lim_{\Delta x \to 0} \frac{\sin \Delta x}{\Delta x} = 1$ and $\lim_{\Delta x \to 0} \frac{\cos \Delta x - 1}{\Delta x} = 0$, the equation collapses beautifully into:

$$\frac{d}{dx}(\sin x) = \cos x$$ $$\frac{d}{dx}(\cos x) = -\sin x$$

Exponential Growth

For $f(x) = a^x$, the limit evaluates to $a^x \lim_{\Delta x \to 0} \frac{a^{\Delta x} - 1}{\Delta x}$. This limit evaluates exactly to the natural logarithm $\ln a$. If we choose Euler's number $e$ as our base, then $\ln e = 1$, revealing the most remarkable property in all of mathematics—it is its own derivative:

$$\frac{d}{dx}(a^x) = a^x \ln a$$ $$\frac{d}{dx}(e^x) = e^x$$

2. The Assembly Line: Product and Quotient Rules

We now have our base gears. But real-world models combine these functions through addition, multiplication, and division. How do we differentiate combinations?

⚙️ The Sum and Constant Rules

  • Sum & Difference: $(f \pm g)'(x) = f'(x) \pm g'(x)$
  • Constant Multiple: $(c \cdot f)'(x) = c \cdot f'(x)$

(The derivative of a sum is simply the sum of the derivatives. You can treat them as completely independent machines[cite: 4].)

However, the derivative of a product is NOT the product of the derivatives. If you have a growing population that is simultaneously increasing its consumption rate, the total consumption does not just scale simply—the two growing factors interact and magnify each other. We must use the Product Rule[cite: 4].

The Product Rule

Let $f$ and $g$ be functions that are differentiable at $x_0$. Then their product $fg$ is differentiable, and:

$$(fg)' = f'g + fg'$$

"Differentiate the first, leave the second alone, PLUS leave the first alone, differentiate the second."

The Quotient Rule

Let $f$ and $g$ be differentiable at $x_0$, and assume $g(x_0) \neq 0$. Then the quotient $\frac{f}{g}$ is differentiable, and:

$$\left(\frac{f}{g}\right)' = \frac{f'g - fg'}{g^2}$$

"Low D-High minus High D-Low, over the square of what's below."

3. Nested Gears: The Chain Rule

The most important rule in differential calculus is the Chain Rule. It allows us to differentiate composite functions—functions nested inside of other functions.

Think of an onion with layers, or a machine nested inside a larger machine. If an inner machine $g(x)$ spins twice as fast as the input $x$, and an outer machine $f(u)$ spins three times as fast as its input $u$, the total system spins $2 \times 3 = 6$ times as fast as the original input. You simply multiply the rates of change together[cite: 4].

The Chain Rule

Let $F(x) = f(g(x))$ be the composition of two functions. If $g$ is differentiable at $x_0$, and $f$ is differentiable at $y_0 = g(x_0)$, then $F(x)$ is differentiable at $x_0$, and:

$$F'(x) = f'(g(x)) \cdot g'(x)$$

"Take the derivative of the OUTSIDE (leaving the inside alone), then MULTIPLY by the derivative of the INSIDE."

4. Reversing the Engine: The Inverse Function Rule

What if we have a function $f(x)$ whose derivative we know, and we want to find the derivative of its inverse function $g(y) = f^{-1}(y)$? Because inverse functions are perfectly mirrored across the line $y = x$, their tangent slopes are perfectly inverted[cite: 4].

The Inverse Function Rule

If $f(x)$ is continuous and differentiable at $x_0$ with $f'(x_0) \neq 0$, and $g(y)$ is the inverse function of $f$ in a neighborhood of $y_0 = f(x_0)$, then $g(y)$ is differentiable at $y_0$ and:

$$g'(y_0) = \frac{1}{f'(x_0)}$$

🎯 Worked Example: The Natural Logarithm

Let $g(y) = \ln y$. We know this is the inverse of the exponential function $f(x) = e^x$.

We know that the derivative of the original function is $f'(x) = e^x$. According to our inverse rule:

$$g'(y) = \frac{1}{f'(x)} = \frac{1}{e^x}$$

But we need the answer in terms of the input $y$. Since $e^x = y$, we substitute $y$ back into the denominator:

$$\frac{d}{dy}(\ln y) = \frac{1}{y}$$
