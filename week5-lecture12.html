Welcome to Lecture 12: The Birth of the Tangent Line

Everything we have learned about limits has been preparing us for a single, profound question. If a function $y = f(x)$ models a process where a quantity changes over time (like a car's distance travelled), we can easily measure its average rate of change by picking two instants of time and dividing the difference.

But what if we want to know the instantaneous rate of change? What does the speedometer say at the exact split-second $x_0$? By fusing the algebra of secant lines with the limit technology we built in Week 4, we will shrink a time interval down to zero and officially give birth to Differential Calculus.

1. The Secant Line: Average Rate of Change

Consider a function $y = f(x)$ that models a process where a measurable quantity $y$ changes over time $x$, such as the distance travelled by a car. If the car moves at a perfectly constant speed, its distance is modeled by a simple linear function, and the rate of change never fluctuates.

But in the real world, processes change at a variable rate. A car accelerates out of a driveway, brakes for a red light, and speeds up on a highway. If we want to find the car's speed over a specific time window—say, between time $x_0$ and time $x_1$—we must compute the average rate of change over that interval.

Average Rate of Change

The average rate of change over the interval $[x_0, x_1]$ is calculated by taking the change in the function's output and dividing it by the time elapsed:

$$m_{av} = \frac{f(x_1) - f(x_0)}{x_1 - x_0}$$

Geometrically, if you plot the start point $(x_0, f(x_0))$ and the end point $(x_1, f(x_1))$ on the curve, $m_{av}$ represents the exact slope of the straight line passing directly through them. This straight line connecting two points on a graph is called a secant line.

Why is it called a secant line? The term comes from the Latin secare, meaning "to cut." While a tangent line merely grazes the outside of a curve at a single point, a secant line actively cuts through the curve, bridging two distinct locations. Because it physically slices across the arc from the start to the end of a journey, its slope perfectly captures the average rate of change between those two moments, completely bypassing any peaks, valleys, or fluctuations that happen in between.

🚗 The Physical Meaning of the Secant

If you calculate the average rate of change over a trip with varying speeds, the slope of the secant line tells you the constant velocity you would have needed to travel to cover the exact same distance in the exact same time. It is a single, flat average that ignores all the actual stops and accelerations along the way.

2. The Limit Definition of the Derivative

As you saw in the animation, if we let the second point slide perfectly on top of the first point, the secant line becomes the tangent line. But there is a massive algebraic roadblock here: The 0/0 Paradox.

⚠️ The Division by Zero Problem

If we want the instantaneous speed at exactly $x_0$, why not just plug $x_1 = x_0$ into our average speed formula? $$m_{av} = \frac{f(x_0) - f(x_0)}{x_0 - x_0} = \frac{0}{0}$$ The formula explodes. We are asking the math to divide by zero, which is undefined. This paradox is why it took humanity thousands of years to invent calculus! We cannot actually reach a distance of $0$; we must use a limit to see what the slope is approaching as the distance shrinks infinitely close to $0$.

By defining $\Delta x = x_1 - x_0$ (meaning "the change in $x$"), we can rewrite our slope formula. Instead of two separate points $x_0$ and $x_1$, we now have our starting point $x$ and a tiny step forward $x + \Delta x$. This gives us the formal definition of the derivative.

How do we actually write this as a mathematical formula? We start with the classic slope formula from algebra: $$m = \frac{y_1 - y_0}{x_1 - x_0}$$

Instead of defining two completely separate points $x_0$ and $x_1$, let's reframe the journey. Let our starting point simply be $x$. We then take a tiny step forward along the horizontal axis. We will call the size of this step $\Delta x$ (literally meaning "change in $x$"). That makes our second point $x + \Delta x$.

  • The Start Point: $(x, f(x))$
  • The End Point: $(x + \Delta x, f(x + \Delta x))$

Now, plug those coordinates directly into the slope formula:

  • The numerator ("rise") becomes $f(x + \Delta x) - f(x)$.
  • The denominator ("run") becomes $(x + \Delta x) - x$. The $x$'s cancel out beautifully, leaving just the step size $\Delta x$.

Apply the limit to this simplified fraction, driving that step size to zero, and we have the most important formula in calculus.

The Limit Definition of the Derivative

The derivative of a function $f(x)$, denoted $f'(x)$, is the exact instantaneous rate of change (the slope of the tangent line) at point $x$. It is defined as:

$$f'(x) = \lim_{\Delta x \to 0} \frac{f(x + \Delta x) - f(x)}{\Delta x}$$

Note: Many textbooks use the letter $h$ instead of $\Delta x$ simply because it is easier to write. Both mean exactly the same thing: a small horizontal step along the x-axis.

Let's see this engine work in practice. We will calculate the derivative of the parabola $f(x) = x^2$ from first principles (meaning directly from the limit definition, without using any shortcuts).

🎯 Worked Example: Differentiating $f(x) = x^2$

Step 1: Set up the limit formula.

Substitute our specific function $f(x) = x^2$ into the definition:

$$f'(x) = \lim_{\Delta x \to 0} \frac{(x + \Delta x)^2 - x^2}{\Delta x}$$

Step 2: Expand the algebra.

Multiply out the $(x + \Delta x)^2$ term:

$$f'(x) = \lim_{\Delta x \to 0} \frac{(x^2 + 2x\Delta x + (\Delta x)^2) - x^2}{\Delta x}$$

Step 3: Cancel and factor.

The $x^2$ and $-x^2$ perfectly cancel each other out. We are left with terms that all share a $\Delta x$, allowing us to factor it out and cancel the denominator (dodging the $0/0$ trap!).

$$f'(x) = \lim_{\Delta x \to 0} \frac{\Delta x(2x + \Delta x)}{\Delta x}$$
$$f'(x) = \lim_{\Delta x \to 0} (2x + \Delta x)$$

Step 4: Evaluate the limit.

Now that the division-by-zero threat is gone, we can safely let $\Delta x$ shrink to exactly $0$:

$$f'(x) = 2x + 0 = 2x$$

Conclusion: For the curve $y = x^2$, the exact steepness of the tangent line at any point $x$ is simply double the x-coordinate!

3. Differentials and Linear Approximation

Let's take a deep breath. The words "linear approximation" and "differentials" sound incredibly intimidating, but they are actually based on one of the most comforting and intuitive ideas in all of mathematics.

Think about standing in an open field. We know the Earth is a sphere, but to your eyes, the ground looks perfectly flat. Why? Because you are zoomed in so incredibly close to the surface that the curvature becomes invisible.

🔍 The Principle of Local Linearity

Calculus relies on this exact same optical illusion. If you take a smooth, complicated, bending curve and zoom in closely enough on a single point, the curve will look like a perfectly straight line. That straight line is the tangent line we just worked so hard to find.

Why is this useful? Because curves are hard to calculate, but straight lines are easy. If we want to find a difficult value on a curve, we can use the easy, straight tangent line to approximate (guess) the answer. As long as we don't step too far away from our starting point, our straight-line guess will be incredibly accurate.

Decoding the Notation: $\Delta y$ vs $dy$

When we take a small step forward (which we call $dx$), we have two different "heights" to think about:

  • The True Change ($\Delta y$): How much the actual, physical curve went up or down. This is often very hard to calculate without a calculator.
  • The Estimated Change ($dy$): How much our straight tangent line went up or down. We call this estimated change the differential.
$$dy = \text{Slope} \times \text{Step Size}$$ $$dy = f'(x) \cdot dx$$

Instead of dealing with a nightmare equation, we simply take our current derivative (the slope), multiply it by our tiny step forward ($dx$), and it tells us roughly how much the function will grow or shrink! Let's see this reassuring shortcut in action.

🎯 Worked Example: Estimating $\sqrt{4.1}$ without a calculator

Imagine you are taking a test, and you need to calculate the square root of $4.1$. You don't have a calculator. The true function is $f(x) = \sqrt{x}$, which is a complicated curve.

Step 1: Start at a safe, known location.

We don't know $\sqrt{4.1}$, but we do know $\sqrt{4}$. So, we anchor our tangent line at $x = 4$. At this point, the height is exactly $2$.

Step 2: Find the slope (the derivative).

The derivative of $f(x) = x^{1/2}$ is $f'(x) = \frac{1}{2\sqrt{x}}$. If we plug in our safe starting point $x=4$, the slope is $\frac{1}{2\sqrt{4}} = \frac{1}{4} = 0.25$.

Step 3: Calculate the Differential ($dy$).

We are taking a tiny step from $x = 4$ to $x = 4.1$. So our step size $dx = 0.1$. How much height do we gain on the straight tangent line?

$$dy = f'(x) \cdot dx$$ $$dy = 0.25 \cdot 0.1 = 0.025$$

Step 4: Make the final estimate.

We started at a height of $2$, and our straight-line estimate says we should gain $0.025$. Therefore, our approximated guess is $2 + 0.025 = 2.025$.

The Reveal: If you type $\sqrt{4.1}$ into a computer, the exact true answer is $2.02484...$ By simply zooming in and riding the straight tangent line, we got the answer right using basic multiplication!

4. When Differentiability Fails

Is every continuous function differentiable? No. In order to have a single, well-defined tangent line, the function must be "smooth". Sharp jagged corners break the machinery.

⚠️ Example: The Absolute Value Cusp

Consider $f(x) = |x|$ at $x = 0$. The function is perfectly continuous (the graph doesn't break).

If we evaluate the limit definition from the right ($h \to 0^+$), the slope is strictly $+1$. But if we evaluate from the left ($h \to 0^-$), the slope is strictly $-1$.

Because $+1 \neq -1$, the limit does not exist. The absolute value function is not differentiable at zero. A physical system modeled by this curve would experience an infinite jolt of acceleration to instantly change direction.

Summary: The Launchpad to Calculus

By shrinking the interval of a secant line down to exactly zero, we utilized limits to resolve the $0/0$ paradox, mathematically securing the instantaneous rate of change.

🎓 Key Takeaways from Lecture 12

  • Secant to Tangent: The average rate of change on an interval shrinks into the instantaneous rate of change at a single point.
  • The Limit Definition: $f'(x_0) = \lim_{\Delta x \to 0} \frac{f(x_0 + \Delta x) - f(x_0)}{\Delta x}$.
  • Smoothness is Required: A function can be continuous but still fail to be differentiable if it possesses a sharp corner or cusp, as the left-hand and right-hand limits of the derivative will not match.
