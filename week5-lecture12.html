Week 5, Lecture 12: Rates of Change & Derivatives

Welcome to Lecture 12: The Birth of the Tangent Line

Everything we have learned about limits has been preparing us for a single, profound question. If a function $y = f(x)$ models a process where a quantity changes over time (like a car's distance travelled), we can easily measure its average rate of change by picking two instants of time and dividing the difference.

But what if we want to know the instantaneous rate of change? What does the speedometer say at the exact split-second $x_0$? By fusing the algebra of secant lines with the limit technology we built in Week 4, we will shrink a time interval down to zero and officially give birth to Differential Calculus.

1. The Secant Line: Average Rate of Change

Consider a function $y = f(x)$ that models a process where a measurable quantity $y$ changes over time $x$, such as the distance travelled by a car. If the car moves at a perfectly constant speed, its distance is modeled by a simple linear function, and the rate of change never fluctuates.

But in the real world, processes change at a variable rate. A car accelerates out of a driveway, brakes for a red light, and speeds up on a highway. If we want to find the car's speed over a specific time window—say, between time $x_0$ and time $x_1$—we must compute the average rate of change over that interval.

Average Rate of Change

The average rate of change over the interval $[x_0, x_1]$ is calculated by taking the change in the function's output and dividing it by the time elapsed:

$$m_{av} = \frac{f(x_1) - f(x_0)}{x_1 - x_0}$$

Geometrically, if you plot the start point $(x_0, f(x_0))$ and the end point $(x_1, f(x_1))$ on the curve, $m_{av}$ represents the exact slope of the straight line passing directly through them. This straight line connecting two points on a graph is called a secant line.

Why is it called a secant line? The term comes from the Latin secare, meaning "to cut." While a tangent line merely grazes the outside of a curve at a single point, a secant line actively cuts through the curve, bridging two distinct locations. Because it physically slices across the arc from the start to the end of a journey, its slope perfectly captures the average rate of change between those two moments, completely bypassing any peaks, valleys, or fluctuations that happen in between.

🚗 The Physical Meaning of the Secant

If you calculate the average rate of change over a trip with varying speeds, the slope of the secant line tells you the constant velocity you would have needed to travel to cover the exact same distance in the exact same time. It is a single, flat average that ignores all the actual stops and accelerations along the way.

2. The Limit Definition of the Derivative

As you saw in the animation, if we let the second point slide perfectly on top of the first point, the secant line becomes the tangent line. But there is a massive algebraic roadblock here: The 0/0 Paradox.

⚠️ The Division by Zero Problem

If we want the instantaneous speed at exactly $x_0$, why not just plug $x_1 = x_0$ into our average speed formula? $$m_{av} = \frac{f(x_0) - f(x_0)}{x_0 - x_0} = \frac{0}{0}$$ The formula explodes. We are asking the math to divide by zero, which is undefined. This paradox is why it took humanity thousands of years to invent calculus! We cannot actually reach a distance of $0$; we must use a limit to see what the slope is approaching as the distance shrinks infinitely close to $0$.

By defining $\Delta x = x_1 - x_0$ (meaning "the change in $x$"), we can rewrite our slope formula. Instead of two separate points $x_0$ and $x_1$, we now have our starting point $x$ and a tiny step forward $x + \Delta x$. This gives us the formal definition of the derivative.

How do we actually write this as a mathematical formula? We start with the classic slope formula from algebra: $$m = \frac{y_1 - y_0}{x_1 - x_0}$$

Instead of defining two completely separate points $x_0$ and $x_1$, let's reframe the journey. Let our starting point simply be $x$. We then take a tiny step forward along the horizontal axis. We will call the size of this step $\Delta x$ (literally meaning "change in $x$"). That makes our second point $x + \Delta x$.

  • The Start Point: $(x, f(x))$
  • The End Point: $(x + \Delta x, f(x + \Delta x))$

Now, plug those coordinates directly into the slope formula:

  • The numerator ("rise") becomes $f(x + \Delta x) - f(x)$.
  • The denominator ("run") becomes $(x + \Delta x) - x$. The $x$'s cancel out beautifully, leaving just the step size $\Delta x$.

Apply the limit to this simplified fraction, driving that step size to zero, and we have the most important formula in calculus.

The Limit Definition of the Derivative

The derivative of a function $f(x)$, denoted $f'(x)$, is the exact instantaneous rate of change (the slope of the tangent line) at point $x$. It is defined as:

$$f'(x) = \lim_{\Delta x \to 0} \frac{f(x + \Delta x) - f(x)}{\Delta x}$$

Note: Many textbooks use the letter $h$ instead of $\Delta x$ simply because it is easier to write. Both mean exactly the same thing: a small horizontal step along the x-axis.

Let's see this engine work in practice. We will calculate the derivative of the parabola $f(x) = x^2$ from first principles (meaning directly from the limit definition, without using any shortcuts).

Historical Context

A Two-Century Journey to the Limit

Calculus Crisis Mathematical Rigor

The limit definition of the derivative developed over two centuries as mathematicians struggled to fix a massive logical flaw at the heart of early calculus.

Late 1600s
The Invention and the Paradox

When Isaac Newton and Gottfried Wilhelm Leibniz independently developed calculus, they built it on "infinitesimals"—quantities that were infinitely small but not quite zero. Leibniz introduced the notation $dx$ and $dy$ to represent these tiny steps. To calculate a tangent slope, they divided a tiny change in $y$ by a tiny change in $x$, and then simply ignored the remaining infinitesimals. They could not logically explain how a number could be non-zero during the division step, but essentially zero at the end.

1734
The Philosophical Attack

This logical gap sparked a crisis. The philosopher Bishop George Berkeley published a famous critique of calculus called The Analyst, attacking the hypocritical math of infinitesimals. He mocked these vanishing increments as the "ghosts of departed quantities," pointing out that true mathematics could not rest on a division-by-zero paradox.

1765
The Conceptual Pivot

The French mathematician Jean Le Rond d'Alembert was one of the first to formally argue that calculus should abandon infinitesimals entirely. He proposed that the derivative should be understood as a limit—the specific, fixed value that the secant slope approaches as the interval shrinks. However, the mathematical tools of the time were not advanced enough to translate his idea into an airtight algebraic proof.

1820s
The Algebraic Definition

Augustin-Louis Cauchy successfully bridged the gap. He provided the first rigorous algebraic definition of the derivative based entirely on limits. Cauchy formally wrote out the difference quotient $\frac{f(x+h) - f(x)}{h}$ and defined the derivative as the limit of this fraction as $h \to 0$, establishing the exact formula taught today.

1870s
The Airtight Foundation

While Cauchy formalized the formula, it was Karl Weierstrass who perfected the underlying logic of the limit itself. Weierstrass invented the epsilon-delta ($\epsilon-\delta$) definition of the limit. By framing limits strictly in terms of absolute inequalities and precise distances, Weierstrass proved exactly how a gap can approach zero mathematically without ever actually being divided by zero, cementing the limit definition as mathematically flawless.

🎯 Worked Example: Differentiating $f(x) = x^2$

Step 1: Set up the limit formula.

Substitute our specific function $f(x) = x^2$ into the definition:

$$f'(x) = \lim_{\Delta x \to 0} \frac{(x + \Delta x)^2 - x^2}{\Delta x}$$

Step 2: Expand the algebra.

Multiply out the $(x + \Delta x)^2$ term:

$$f'(x) = \lim_{\Delta x \to 0} \frac{(x^2 + 2x\Delta x + (\Delta x)^2) - x^2}{\Delta x}$$

Step 3: Cancel and factor.

The $x^2$ and $-x^2$ perfectly cancel each other out. We are left with terms that all share a $\Delta x$, allowing us to factor it out and cancel the denominator (dodging the $0/0$ trap!).

$$f'(x) = \lim_{\Delta x \to 0} \frac{\Delta x(2x + \Delta x)}{\Delta x}$$
$$f'(x) = \lim_{\Delta x \to 0} (2x + \Delta x)$$

Step 4: Evaluate the limit.

Now that the division-by-zero threat is gone, we can safely let $\Delta x$ shrink to exactly $0$:

$$f'(x) = 2x + 0 = 2x$$

Conclusion: For the curve $y = x^2$, the exact steepness of the tangent line at any point $x$ is simply double the x-coordinate!

3. Differentials and Linear Approximation

Let's take a deep breath. The words "linear approximation" and "differentials" sound incredibly intimidating, but they are actually based on one of the most comforting and intuitive ideas in all of mathematics.

Think about standing in an open field. We know the Earth is a sphere, but to your eyes, the ground looks perfectly flat. Why? Because you are zoomed in so incredibly close to the surface that the curvature becomes invisible.

🔍 The Principle of Local Linearity

Calculus relies on this exact same optical illusion. If you take a smooth, complicated, bending curve and zoom in closely enough on a single point, the curve will look like a perfectly straight line. That straight line is the tangent line we just worked so hard to find.

Why is this useful? Because curves are hard to calculate, but straight lines are easy. If we want to find a difficult value on a curve, we can use the easy, straight tangent line to approximate (guess) the answer. As long as we don't step too far away from our starting point, our straight-line guess will be incredibly accurate.

Decoding the Notation: $\Delta y$ vs $dy$

When we take a small step forward (which we call $dx$), we have two different "heights" to think about:

  • The True Change ($\Delta y$): How much the actual, physical curve went up or down. This is often very hard to calculate without a calculator.
  • The Estimated Change ($dy$): How much our straight tangent line went up or down. We call this estimated change the differential.
$$dy = \text{Slope} \times \text{Step Size}$$ $$dy = f'(x) \cdot dx$$

Instead of dealing with a nightmare equation, we simply take our current derivative (the slope), multiply it by our tiny step forward ($dx$), and it tells us roughly how much the function will grow or shrink! Let's see this reassuring shortcut in action.

🎯 Worked Example: Estimating $\sqrt{4.1}$ without a calculator

Imagine you are taking a test, and you need to calculate the square root of $4.1$. You don't have a calculator. The true function is $f(x) = \sqrt{x}$, which is a complicated curve.

Step 1: Start at a safe, known location.

We don't know $\sqrt{4.1}$, but we do know $\sqrt{4}$. So, we anchor our tangent line at $x = 4$. At this point, the height is exactly $2$.

Step 2: Find the slope (the derivative).

The derivative of $f(x) = x^{1/2}$ is $f'(x) = \frac{1}{2\sqrt{x}}$. If we plug in our safe starting point $x=4$, the slope is $\frac{1}{2\sqrt{4}} = \frac{1}{4} = 0.25$.

Step 3: Calculate the Differential ($dy$).

We are taking a tiny step from $x = 4$ to $x = 4.1$. So our step size $dx = 0.1$. How much height do we gain on the straight tangent line?

$$dy = f'(x) \cdot dx$$ $$dy = 0.25 \cdot 0.1 = 0.025$$

Step 4: Make the final estimate.

We started at a height of $2$, and our straight-line estimate says we should gain $0.025$. Therefore, our approximated guess is $2 + 0.025 = 2.025$.

The Reveal: If you type $\sqrt{4.1}$ into a computer, the exact true answer is $2.02484...$ By simply zooming in and riding the straight tangent line, we got the answer right using basic multiplication!

5. When Differentiability Fails

Up until now, we have assumed that every continuous curve has a neat, easily defined tangent line. But is this always true? If a function doesn't have any breaks or gaps in it, can we always find its derivative?

The answer is no. In order for a derivative to exist at a specific point, the graph must be "smooth." Think about balancing a flat, straight ruler (the tangent line) on top of a curve. If you balance a ruler on the top of a smooth dome, it sits perfectly flat. There is only one stable way it can rest. But if you try to balance a ruler on the sharp tip of a pyramid, it wobbles. You could tilt it in dozens of different directions, and none of them would be the single, definitive "true" tangent line.

The "One True Tangent" Rule

Mathematically, a derivative is a limit. And remember our golden rule from Week 4: A limit only exists if the left side and the right side agree. If the calculated slope coming from the left is different from the calculated slope coming from the right, the derivative explodes and does not exist.

Let's look at the most famous example of a function that is perfectly connected, but impossible to differentiate at its center.

⚠️ Example: The Absolute Value Cusp

Consider the absolute value function, $f(x) = |x|$. If you draw this graph, it forms a sharp "V" shape whose lowest point hits exactly at the origin $(0,0)$. The line is completely continuous—you can draw it without lifting your pencil. But let's look at the slopes.

Approaching from the Left (Negative numbers):

If we look at the graph before zero, it is a straight line pointing diagonally downward. The slope is strictly $-1$.

Approaching from the Right (Positive numbers):

If we look at the graph after zero, it is a straight line pointing diagonally upward. The slope is strictly $+1$.

The Collision at $x = 0$:

What is the exact slope at the tip of the "V" where $x = 0$? The left-hand limit is screaming that the slope is $-1$. The right-hand limit is screaming that the slope is $+1$. Because $-1 \neq +1$, the two sides fundamentally disagree.

Conclusion: Because the limit fails, the absolute value function is not differentiable at zero. If you try to calculate $f'(0)$, the answer is mathematically undefined.

🚗 The Physical Reality of a Sharp Corner

Why does the math break here? Imagine the graph $f(x) = |x|$ represents a car's physical position. The left side means the car is driving in reverse at 1 meter per second. The right side means the car is driving forward at 1 meter per second.

At exactly time $t = 0$, the sharp corner dictates that the car must instantly switch from driving backwards to driving forwards in zero seconds. There is no curve where the car slows down, stops, and accelerates forward. To change direction instantly requires an infinite jolt of force (infinite acceleration), which breaks the laws of physics. That is why sharp corners simply cannot have a defined instantaneous rate of change!

The Mathematical Monster: How to Break Differentiability

To prove that continuity does not guarantee differentiability, Karl Weierstrass needed to invent a curve that possessed two deeply conflicting physical properties at the exact same time: it had to be a single, unbroken wire (continuous), but every microscopic millimeter of that wire had to be a sharp, jagged corner (nowhere differentiable).

He achieved this by summing an infinite series of cosine waves:

$$f(x) = \sum_{n=0}^{\infty} a^n \cos(b^n \pi x)$$

The Architecture of the Equation:

  • The Shrinking Amplitude ($0 < a < 1$): Because $a$ is a fraction, $a^n$ rapidly shrinks toward zero as $n$ grows. Each new wave added to the sum is shorter than the last. This guarantees the infinite sum converges cleanly, ensuring the final curve has no physical breaks, jumps, or asymptotes (establishing continuity).
  • The Exploding Frequency ($b$ is an odd integer $> 1$): The term $b^n$ multiplies the frequency, making each successive wave vibrate wildly faster than the one before it.
  • The Fatal Imbalance ($ab > 1 + \frac{3}{2}\pi$): This is Weierstrass's stroke of genius. The frequency parameter $b$ grows significantly faster than the amplitude parameter $a$ shrinks. Therefore, the steepness (the derivative) of each new layer is larger than the previous layer. The zig-zags get tighter much faster than they get shorter, weaponizing infinity to shatter any attempt to draw a flat tangent line (destroying differentiability).

Historical Context

The Weierstrass Monster & The Collapse of Intuition

Mathematical Crisis Real Analysis

The history of the Weierstrass Monster—more formally known as the Weierstrass function—is one of the most disruptive chapters in the history of mathematics, representing the exact moment 19th-century mathematicians realized that human geometric intuition could no longer be trusted.

Late 1600s – 1806
1. The Pre-Weierstrass Landscape: An Intuitive Illusion

For centuries following the invention of calculus by Isaac Newton and Gottfried Wilhelm Leibniz, mathematics was heavily tethered to physical geometry and motion. Mathematicians viewed functions primarily as curves that could be drawn smoothly with a pencil.

Because of this visual bias, a deep-seated assumption took root: If a function is continuous (meaning its graph has no breaks, jumps, or gaps), it must be differentiable (meaning it has a smooth, measurable slope) almost everywhere. The belief was so strong that in 1806, the prominent French physicist and mathematician André-Marie Ampère even published a formal "proof" attempting to show that every continuous function must have a derivative everywhere, save for a few isolated points. For decades, the mathematical community accepted this as an absolute truth.

c. 1830
2. The Hidden Precursor

Unbeknownst to the broader mathematical world, the first crack in the foundation was actually struck around 1830 by Bernard Bolzano, a Czech priest and mathematician working in intellectual isolation in Prague. Bolzano constructed the very first mathematical example of a function that was continuous everywhere yet lacked a derivative at any point.

However, Bolzano’s work was far ahead of its time, and he never published it. His manuscript sat quietly in an archive and was not discovered until 1930—long after the mathematical world had already been upended.

July 18, 1872
3. Weierstrass Unveils the Monster

The true turning point came when the German mathematician Karl Weierstrass read a paper before the Royal Prussian Academy of Sciences in Berlin. Weierstrass presented an explicit, algebraic formula built from an infinite series of cosine waves:

$$f(x) = \sum_{n=0}^{\infty} a^n \cos(b^n \pi x)$$

By ensuring that the frequency grew much faster than the amplitude shrank ($ab > 1$), Weierstrass created a fractal curve that zig-zagged infinitely at every level of magnification. He proved rigorously that his function possessed two terrifying properties:

  • It is continuous everywhere: You can trace the entire curve without ever lifting your pen.
  • It is differentiable nowhere: Every single point on the curve is a sharp, infinitely acute corner, meaning a tangent line can never be drawn.
Post-1872
4. Why It Was So Controversial

When Weierstrass unleashed his function, it caused an absolute crisis in the mathematical community. It challenged the philosophical underpinnings of how people understood math:

  • An "Outrage Against Common Sense": Mathematicians pride themselves on logic, but 19th-century math was still propped up by what people could picture in their minds. The French mathematician Henri Poincaré later lamented that these types of functions were created precisely to "disgrace our ordinary reasoning," calling them an outrage against common sense.
  • The "Lamentable Evil": Other contemporaries viewed these kinds of pathological curves not as brilliant discoveries, but as infections threatening the purity of calculus. The legendary French mathematician Charles Hermite wrote a famous letter to a colleague expressing his disgust: "I turn away with fright and horror from this lamentable evil of functions which do not have derivatives."
  • The Destruction of Geometric Calculus: Until then, calculus teachers could tell students, "Just look at the curve; of course it has a slope." Weierstrass proved that a formula could describe a completely unbroken curve that defied all physical drawing and geometric reasoning. It proved that human eyes are blind to the true behavior of infinity.
Late 19th Century & Beyond
5. The Legacy: Birth of Rigorous Real Analysis

Ultimately, the Weierstrass Monster forced a revolution in mathematics known as the rigor movement or the arithmetization of analysis.

Instead of relying on sloppy drawings, hand-waving, and notions of physical motion, mathematicians realized they had to build calculus entirely out of static, bulletproof arithmetic logic—relying on precise definitions, set theory, and the $\epsilon\text{-}\delta$ framework. What was initially treated as a bizarre, offensive "monster" ended up saving mathematics from its own logical complacency, paving the way for modern topology, fractal geometry, and advanced analysis.

Summary: The Launchpad to Calculus

By shrinking the interval of a secant line down to exactly zero, we utilized limits to resolve the $0/0$ paradox, mathematically securing the instantaneous rate of change.

🎓 Key Takeaways from Lecture 12

  • Secant to Tangent: The average rate of change on an interval shrinks into the instantaneous rate of change at a single point.
  • The Limit Definition: $f'(x_0) = \lim_{\Delta x \to 0} \frac{f(x_0 + \Delta x) - f(x_0)}{\Delta x}$.
  • Smoothness is Required: A function can be continuous but still fail to be differentiable if it possesses a sharp corner or cusp, as the left-hand and right-hand limits of the derivative will not match.
