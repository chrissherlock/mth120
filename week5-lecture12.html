Week 5, Lecture 12: Rates of Change & Derivatives

Welcome to Lecture 12: The Birth of the Tangent Line

Everything we have learned about limits has been preparing us for a single, profound question. If a function $y = f(x)$ models a process where a quantity changes over time (like a car's distance travelled), we can easily measure its average rate of change by picking two instants of time and dividing the difference.

But what if we want to know the instantaneous rate of change? What does the speedometer say at the exact split-second $x_0$? By fusing the algebra of secant lines with the limit technology we built in Week 4, we will shrink a time interval down to zero and officially give birth to Differential Calculus.

1. The Secant Line: Average Rate of Change

Consider a process that changes at a variable rate, like an object falling due to gravity. The height $y$ at any time $t$ follows a curve, not a straight line. If we want to find the object's speed between two times $x_0$ and $x_1$, we compute the average rate of change.

Average Rate of Change

The average rate of change over the interval $[x_0, x_1]$ is calculated as:

$$m_{av} = \frac{f(x_1) - f(x_0)}{x_1 - x_0}$$

Geometrically, $m_{av}$ is the slope of the straight line passing through the points $(x_0, f(x_0))$ and $(x_1, f(x_1))$. Such a straight line connecting two points on a graph is called a secant line.

What Is Happening

The interval $\Delta x$ is $2.0$. The average slope $m_{av} = \frac{3^2 - 1^2}{3 - 1} = \frac{8}{2} = 4.0$.

Why The System Does This

Because $\Delta x$ is so large, the straight secant line deeply misses the true curvature of the physical system. It is a crude average of the journey.

3. The Limit Definition of the Derivative

As you saw in the interactive lab, if we let the second point $x_1$ approach the first point $x_0$ without ever actually touching it (which would trigger a $\frac{0}{0}$ explosion), the secant line perfectly resolves into the tangent line.

The Derivative of a Function

If the limit exists, we say the function $f$ is differentiable at $x_0$, and the instantaneous rate of change is defined as:

$$m = \lim_{x_1 \to x_0} \frac{f(x_1) - f(x_0)}{x_1 - x_0}$$

By defining the increment $\Delta x = x_1 - x_0$, this famous limit is more commonly written as: $$f'(x_0) = \lim_{\Delta x \to 0} \frac{f(x_0 + \Delta x) - f(x_0)}{\Delta x}$$

4. Differentials and Linear Approximation

Once we have the tangent line, it serves a secondary, powerful purpose. Because a smooth curve looks increasingly like a straight line the closer you zoom in, we can use the linear tangent equation to approximate complicated non-linear functions locally.

The tangent line equation is $y = l(x) = y_0 + m(x - x_0)$. We capture this linear shift using differentials.

⚙️ The Differential $df$

The differential $df$ represents the theoretical change along the perfect tangent line (unlike $\Delta f$, which is the actual change on the curve).

$$df = m \, dx$$

This gives rise to the classic Leibniz notation for the derivative: $\frac{df}{dx} = m$.

5. When Differentiability Fails

Is every continuous function differentiable? No. In order to have a single, well-defined tangent line, the function must be "smooth". Sharp jagged corners break the machinery.

⚠️ Example: The Absolute Value Cusp

Consider $f(x) = |x|$ at $x = 0$. The function is perfectly continuous (the graph doesn't break).

If we evaluate the limit definition from the right ($h \to 0^+$), the slope is strictly $+1$. But if we evaluate from the left ($h \to 0^-$), the slope is strictly $-1$.

Because $+1 \neq -1$, the limit does not exist. The absolute value function is not differentiable at zero. A physical system modeled by this curve would experience an infinite jolt of acceleration to instantly change direction.

Summary: The Launchpad to Calculus

By shrinking the interval of a secant line down to exactly zero, we utilized limits to resolve the $0/0$ paradox, mathematically securing the instantaneous rate of change.

🎓 Key Takeaways from Lecture 12

  • Secant to Tangent: The average rate of change on an interval shrinks into the instantaneous rate of change at a single point.
  • The Limit Definition: $f'(x_0) = \lim_{\Delta x \to 0} \frac{f(x_0 + \Delta x) - f(x_0)}{\Delta x}$.
  • Smoothness is Required: A function can be continuous but still fail to be differentiable if it possesses a sharp corner or cusp, as the left-hand and right-hand limits of the derivative will not match.
