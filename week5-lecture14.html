Week 5, Lecture 14: Monotone Functions & Concavity

Welcome to Lecture 14: Reading the Telemetry of a Curve

In Lecture 13, you mastered the mechanical rules of differentiation. You can now take a raw mathematical equation and instantly generate a new formula representing its rate of change. But what does that new formula actually tell us about the original physical system?

Today, we step back from the algebra and return to the geometry. By analyzing the signs (positive or negative) of a function's first and second derivatives, you can perfectly reconstruct the physical shape of its graph without ever plotting points. The derivatives act as a continuous telemetry stream, telling us exactly where the curve is climbing, falling, peaking, or bending.

🗺️ The Three Layers of Telemetry
  • The Position $f(x)$: Tells us exactly where the system is at a given moment.
  • The Velocity $f'(x)$: Tells us if the system is increasing (moving up) or decreasing (moving down).
  • The Acceleration $f''(x)$: Tells us if the system is bending upward (concave up) or bending downward (concave down).

1. The First Derivative: Tracking Increase & Decrease

Before we use calculus to analyze a curve, we need a precise way to describe what it means for a function to climb or fall. Intuitively, a function is increasing if its graph rises as you read it from left to right, and decreasing if it falls.

Algebraically, we lock this down by comparing any two inputs $x_1$ and $x_2$ on an interval $I$:

Definition: Monotone Functions

Let $f$ be a function defined on an interval $I$. For any two points $x_1, x_2 \in I$ with $x_1 < x_2$:

  • $f$ is strictly increasing on $I$ if $f(x_1) < f(x_2)$.
  • $f$ is strictly decreasing on $I$ if $f(x_1) > f(x_2)$.
  • A function that is either strictly increasing or strictly decreasing on $I$ is called monotone on $I$.

Testing every possible pair of points $x_1 < x_2$ by hand is impossible. This is where the first derivative $f'(x)$ becomes our compass. Because $f'(x)$ measures the instantaneous slope of the tangent line, its sign tells us which way the curve is heading: a positive slope aims upward, and a negative slope aims downward.

Theorem 14: Monotonicity Test

Suppose the function $f$ is continuous on the closed interval $[a, b]$ and differentiable on the open interval $(a, b)$.

  • If $f'(x) > 0$ for all $x \in (a, b)$, then $f$ is strictly increasing on $[a, b]$.
  • If $f'(x) < 0$ for all $x \in (a, b)$, then $f$ is strictly decreasing on $[a, b]$.
  • If $f'(x) = 0$ for all $x \in (a, b)$, then $f$ is constant on $[a, b]$.

The Foundation: Rolle's Theorem

There is a subtle logical leap hidden inside Theorem 14. The derivative $f'(x)$ is a purely local measurement—it only inspects an infinitely tiny neighborhood around a single point $x$. Monotonicity, on the other hand, is a global property—it compares the heights of two separate points, $x_1$ and $x_2$, across an entire interval.

To bridge local slopes to global behavior, we start with the simplest possible case. Imagine throwing a ball straight up into the air and catching it at the exact same height you threw it from ($f(a) = f(b)$). If its flight is smooth and unbroken, there must be at least one instant $c$ in the middle where the ball momentarily stops rising and turns around—meaning its instantaneous velocity is zero ($f'(c) = 0$).

Rolle's Theorem

Suppose a function $f$ satisfies three conditions on an interval $[a, b]$:

  1. $f$ is continuous on the closed interval $[a, b]$.
  2. $f$ is differentiable on the open interval $(a, b)$.
  3. The endpoints are at the exact same height: $f(a) = f(b)$.

Then there exists at least one number $c \in (a, b)$ such that $f'(c) = 0$.

Why must this point $c$ exist? If $f$ is flat the whole way, then $f'(x) = 0$ everywhere. Otherwise, because $f$ is continuous on the closed interval $[a, b]$, it must rise to a peak or dip to a valley somewhere strictly inside $(a, b)$. At that smooth interior peak or valley, the tangent line must flatten out to zero.

📜 Historical Aside: Michel Rolle (1652–1719) — The Reluctant Pillar of Calculus

There is a rich historical irony behind Rolle's Theorem: the man whose name sits at the foundation of differential calculus spent years trying to destroy it. Born in Ambert, France, Michel Rolle had almost no formal university education. He worked as a lowly scribe for notaries and attorneys, teaching himself algebra in his spare time until he solved a notorious number-theory problem in 1682—a feat that won him a royal pension and a seat in the French Academy of Sciences.

When Rolle published his famous theorem in 1691, he did not use derivatives, limits, or geometry. He was working strictly on polynomials, trying to isolate their roots using a purely algebraic technique he called the method of cascades. To Rolle, the newly invented infinitesimal calculus of Leibniz was logically unsound—he publicly denounced it in the Academy as a "collection of ingenious fallacies" and waged a bitter, years-long feud against its defenders.

By 1706, Rolle softened his stance and acknowledged the usefulness of calculus, though he never saw how central his own result would become. More than a century later, mathematicians generalized his polynomial rule to all differentiable functions, turning the skeptic's algebraic tool into the load-bearing proof behind the Mean Value Theorem.

Tilting the Picture: The Mean Value Theorem

Now drop the requirement that $f(a) = f(b)$. Allow the endpoints to sit at any two heights. The straight secant line connecting $(a, f(a))$ and $(b, f(b))$ is no longer flat—it has a tilted slope of $\frac{f(b) - f(a)}{b - a}$.

If we measure the vertical gap between our curve $f(x)$ and that straight secant line, we get a new function $g(x)$ whose endpoints are both at height $0$ (since the curve and secant line meet at $a$ and $b$). Applying Rolle's Theorem to that gap function $g(x)$ guarantees a point $c \in (a, b)$ where $g'(c) = 0$—meaning the curve's slope matches the secant's slope. That tilted version of Rolle's Theorem is the Mean Value Theorem (MVT), first proved by Joseph-Louis Lagrange in 1797 and formalized by Augustin-Louis Cauchy.

The Mean Value Theorem (MVT)

If $f$ is continuous on the closed interval $[a, b]$ and differentiable on the open interval $(a, b)$, then there exists at least one number $c \in (a, b)$ such that:

$$f'(c) = \frac{f(b) - f(a)}{b - a} \quad \Longleftrightarrow \quad f(b) - f(a) = f'(c)(b - a)$$
Geometric & Physical Intuition

The Mean Value Theorem (Average-Speed Cameras)

This is the exact mathematics behind point-to-point average-speed cameras on a highway. If you pass Camera A and arrive at Camera B 100 km away in 50 minutes, your average speed (the secant slope) is 120 km/h. Even if no camera saw you speeding at a specific instant, the MVT legally guarantees that your speedometer (the tangent slope) must have hit at least 120 km/h at some point $c$ along the road.

How this proves Theorem 14: Pick any two points $x_1 < x_2$ inside $[a, b]$. Applying the rearranged Mean Value Theorem to the sub-interval $[x_1, x_2]$ guarantees a point $c \in (x_1, x_2)$ such that:

$$f(x_2) - f(x_1) = f'(c)\underbrace{(x_2 - x_1)}_{> \, 0}$$

Because $x_2 > x_1$, the factor $(x_2 - x_1)$ is strictly positive. Therefore, the sign of the total height change $f(x_2) - f(x_1)$ is dictated entirely by the sign of the local derivative $f'(c)$:

  • If $f'(x) > 0$ everywhere on $(a, b)$, then $f'(c) > 0$, forcing $f(x_2) - f(x_1) > 0 \implies f(x_2) > f(x_1)$ (strictly increasing).
  • If $f'(x) < 0$ everywhere on $(a, b)$, then $f'(c) < 0$, forcing $f(x_2) - f(x_1) < 0 \implies f(x_2) < f(x_1)$ (strictly decreasing).
  • If $f'(x) = 0$ everywhere on $(a, b)$, then $f'(c) = 0$, forcing $f(x_2) - f(x_1) = 0 \implies f(x_2) = f(x_1)$ (constant).

🔍 Why Both Hypotheses Matter (When Rolle's & MVT Break)

Both Rolle's Theorem and the MVT require the function to be continuous on $[a, b]$ and differentiable on $(a, b)$. Drop either condition, and the guarantee collapses:

  • Drop Continuity (Teleportation): A step function that sits at $y = 0$ for $x < 0$ and jumps to $y = 1$ for $x \ge 0$ has a positive average slope across $[-1, 1]$, yet its derivative is $0$ everywhere except the jump. It changed height without ever having a positive slope.
  • Drop Differentiability (Sharp Corners): Consider $f(x) = |x|$ on $[-1, 1]$. It is continuous everywhere and $f(-1) = f(1) = 1$, so it meets Rolle's endpoint condition. Yet the derivative is $-1$ on the left, $+1$ on the right, and undefined at the sharp corner $x = 0$—it never hits $0$ anywhere.

🧮 Worked Example: Finding Intervals of Increase & Decrease

Find the intervals on which $f(x) = x^3 - 3x^2 - 9x + 5$ is increasing and decreasing.

  1. Differentiate: $f'(x) = 3x^2 - 6x - 9$.
  2. Factor & find where $f'(x) = 0$: $$3(x^2 - 2x - 3) = 3(x - 3)(x + 1) = 0 \implies x = -1 \text{ and } x = 3$$ These boundary points slice the real number line into three open intervals: $(-\infty, -1)$, $(-1, 3)$, and $(3, \infty)$.
  3. Build a Sign Chart: Because $f'(x)$ is continuous and only equals zero at $-1$ and $3$, its sign cannot change inside any of those intervals. Pick one easy test point in each interval:
Interval Test Point Sign of $f'(x) = 3(x-3)(x+1)$ Behavior of $f(x)$
$(-\infty, -1)$ $x = -2$ $3(-)(-)= +$ (Positive) Increasing $\nearrow$
$(-1, 3)$ $x = 0$ $3(-)(+)= -$ (Negative) Decreasing $\searrow$
$(3, \infty)$ $x = 4$ $3(+)(+)= +$ (Positive) Increasing $\nearrow$

Conclusion: $f(x)$ is strictly increasing on $(-\infty, -1]$ and $[3, \infty)$, and strictly decreasing on $[-1, 3]$.

⚠️ Two Classic Traps to Watch For

  • Isolated flat spots don't stop a climb: Consider $f(x) = x^3$. Its derivative is $f'(x) = 3x^2$, which equals $0$ at $x = 0$. Even though $f'(0)$ is not strictly positive, $x^3$ is still strictly increasing across the entire real line—it just pauses horizontally for a single instant at the origin before continuing upward.
  • Never ignore discontinuities: Theorem 14 requires $f$ to be continuous on the interval. For $f(x) = -\frac{1}{x}$, the derivative is $f'(x) = \frac{1}{x^2} > 0$ everywhere it exists. Yet $f$ is not increasing across $x = 0$ (for example, $f(-1) = 1 > f(1) = -1$). It is increasing on $(-\infty, 0)$ and on $(0, \infty)$ separately, never across the vertical asymptote.

2. Critical Points & The First Derivative Test

If the first derivative tells us where a curve is climbing and where it is falling, its most practical payoff is locating the exact peaks and valleys—known formally as relative (or local) extrema (extrema is the plural of extremum, a blanket term covering both maxima and minima).

The word local is crucial: a mountain peak doesn't have to be Mount Everest to count as a peak. It only needs to be higher than the terrain immediately surrounding it. To make "immediately surrounding" mathematically precise, we wrap the point $c$ in a small open interval $(c - \delta, c + \delta)$, called a neighborhood:

Definition: Relative (Local) Extrema

Let $c$ be a point in the domain of $f$.

  • $f$ has a relative (local) maximum at $x = c$ if there exists an open interval $(c - \delta, c + \delta)$ around $c$ such that $f(c) \ge f(x)$ for all $x$ in that interval.
  • $f$ has a relative (local) minimum at $x = c$ if there exists an open interval $(c - \delta, c + \delta)$ around $c$ such that $f(c) \le f(x)$ for all $x$ in that interval.

Two distinctions are worth keeping sharp:

  • Local vs. Absolute (Global) Extrema: An absolute maximum is the single highest point across the entire domain of $f$. As the diagram above shows, a function can have a local maximum at $c$ that is still lower than another peak elsewhere—it only has to beat its immediate neighbors inside $(c - \delta, c + \delta)$.
  • Location vs. Value: Be careful with wording. We say the relative maximum occurs at $x = c$, while the maximum value itself is the output height $f(c)$.

To hunt down these peaks and valleys without graphing the function, we first narrow our search to a small list of suspects called critical points.

Stationary vs. Critical Points

Let $c$ be an interior point in the domain of $f$:

  • Stationary Points: Any point where the derivative equals zero ($f'(c) = 0$). The tangent line is completely horizontal.
  • Critical Points: A broader category that includes all stationary points, plus any point in the domain where $f'(c)$ is undefined (such as sharp corners like $f(x) = |x|$ or vertical cusps like $f(x) = x^{2/3}$ at $x = 0$).

Why do we bother with the broader category of critical points instead of just looking for stationary points? Because a function can reverse direction in two fundamentally different ways. A smooth curve has to slow down and momentarily level off ($f'(c) = 0$) before turning around. A non-smooth curve, however, can snap abruptly from falling to rising at a sharp corner or a vertical cusp—producing a genuine peak or valley where no single tangent line exists at all.

Notice the domain requirement in the definition: a point $c$ is only a critical point if $f(c)$ actually exists. If $f'(c)$ is undefined simply because the original function has a vertical asymptote or a hole at $c$, then $c$ is not a critical point—there is no point on the curve there to be a peak or valley. Think of critical points as the complete candidate list for interior extrema: every true interior peak or valley must appear on this list, though not every candidate on the list will turn out to be an extremum.

Fermat's Theorem: Why Extrema Must Be Critical Points

Theorem 18 (Fermat's Theorem): If a function $f$ has a relative extremum at an interior point $x = c$, then $x = c$ must be a critical point (either $f'(c) = 0$ or $f'(c)$ does not exist).

The logic is straightforward: suppose $f'(c)$ existed and were strictly positive ($f'(c) > 0$). That means the curve is actively climbing through $c$—so stepping infinitesimally to the right would land you at a higher point, and stepping to the left would land you at a lower point. Therefore, $c$ could be neither a peak nor a valley. The same argument rules out $f'(c) < 0$. The only way a smooth curve can turn around is by momentarily flattening to $f'(c) = 0$, or by snapping sharply so that no tangent exists at all.

However, the reverse is not true. Finding $f'(c) = 0$ only puts $c$ on the suspect list; it does not convict it as a peak or valley. A curve can flatten out into a horizontal terrace and then keep moving in the exact same direction. To classify each critical point, we check the sign of $f'(x)$ on either side of it.

Diagrammed Example

Why $f'(c) = 0$ Is Not Enough: The Terrace of $f(x) = x^3$

For $f(x) = x^3$, the derivative is $f'(x) = 3x^2$, which equals $0$ at $x = 0$. That makes the origin a stationary point—yet it is neither a peak nor a valley. On the left ($x = -1$), the tangent slopes upward ($f'(-1) = 3 > 0$). At $x = 0$, the curve pauses horizontally for a single instant. On the right ($x = 1$), it resumes climbing ($f'(1) = 3 > 0$).

Theorem 19: The First Derivative Test

Suppose $f$ is continuous at a critical point $x = c$ and differentiable in an open interval around $c$ (except possibly at $c$ itself). As $x$ moves from left to right across $c$:

  • If $f'(x)$ changes from positive to negative ($+ \to -$), the curve rose to a peak and fell: $f(c)$ is a Relative Maximum.
  • If $f'(x)$ changes from negative to positive ($- \to +$), the curve fell into a valley and rose: $f(c)$ is a Relative Minimum.
  • If $f'(x)$ does not change sign ($+ \to +$ or $- \to -$), $c$ is merely a flat terrace—no relative extremum occurs at $c$.

🧮 Worked Example: Classifying Critical Points (Including a False Alarm)

Find and classify all critical points of $f(x) = x^4 - 4x^3$.

  1. Differentiate and factor: $$f'(x) = 4x^3 - 12x^2 = 4x^2(x - 3)$$
  2. Find the critical points: Since $f'(x)$ is a polynomial, it is defined everywhere. Setting $f'(x) = 0$ gives two stationary points: $x = 0$ and $x = 3$.
  3. Test the sign of $f'(x)$ on each interval: Notice that $4x^2$ is always positive away from $0$, so the sign of $f'(x)$ depends solely on $(x - 3)$:
Interval Test Point Sign of $f'(x) = 4x^2(x - 3)$ Behavior of $f(x)$
$(-\infty, 0)$ $x = -1$ $(+)(-) = -$ (Negative) Decreasing $\searrow$
$(0, 3)$ $x = 1$ $(+)(-) = -$ (Negative) Decreasing $\searrow$
$(3, \infty)$ $x = 4$ $(+)(+) = +$ (Positive) Increasing $\nearrow$

Classification:
• At $x = 0$, $f'(x)$ stays negative ($- \to -$). The curve flattens briefly at $(0, 0)$ and keeps falling—a flat terrace, not an extremum.
• At $x = 3$, $f'(x)$ switches from negative to positive ($- \to +$). Therefore, $f$ has a Relative Minimum at $(3, f(3)) = (3, -27)$.

⚠️ Two Common Mistakes with Critical Points

  • Asymptotes are not critical points: A critical point must belong to the domain of $f$. For $f(x) = \frac{1}{x}$, $f'(x) = -\frac{1}{x^2}$ is undefined at $x = 0$, but $x = 0$ is not in the domain of $f$—it is a vertical asymptote, not a critical point. You should still include it as a divider on your sign chart, because $f'(x)$ can change sign across an asymptote.
  • Don't forget non-differentiable corners: If you only solve $f'(x) = 0$, you will miss sharp peaks and valleys like $f(x) = |x|$ at $x = 0$. The First Derivative Test still works on them: $f'(x)$ jumps from $-1$ to $+1$ across $x = 0$, confirming a relative minimum at the corner.

3. The Second Derivative: Bending and Concavity

Velocity (the first derivative) tells us if we are moving up or down. But what if we track the rate of change of the rate of change? Taking the derivative of the derivative yields the Second Derivative, denoted $f''(x)$ or $\frac{d^2f}{dx^2}$.

In physics, this is acceleration. In geometry, this tracks the structural bending of the curve, known as concavity.

Concave Up ($f''(x) > 0$)

The slopes are strictly increasing. The curve bends upward like a bowl holding water. Any secant line connecting two points on this section will physically lie above the graph.

Concave Down ($f''(x) < 0$)

The slopes are strictly decreasing. The curve bends downward like an umbrella. Any secant line connecting two points on this section will physically lie below the graph.

🔀 Points of Inflection

What happens at the exact boundary where a curve transitions from being a bowl (concave up) to an umbrella (concave down)? The structural bending briefly zeroes out. If the function has a continuous second derivative across this boundary, it must be that $f''(x) = 0$. These transitional boundaries are called Points of Inflection.

4. The Second Derivative Test

Because the second derivative maps out the "bowls" and "umbrellas" of a function, it gives us a lightning-fast alternative for classifying stationary points without having to check points on either side.

Theorem 20: The Second Derivative Test

Assume $f$ is twice differentiable at a stationary point $x = c$ (meaning $f'(c) = 0$).

  • If $f''(c) > 0$, the curve is a bowl bending upward. The flat spot at $c$ must be the bottom of the bowl: a Relative Minimum.
  • If $f''(c) < 0$, the curve is an umbrella bending downward. The flat spot at $c$ must be the top of the umbrella: a Relative Maximum.
  • If $f''(c) = 0$, the test is inconclusive. You must revert to the First Derivative Test to manually check the slopes on either side.

The Shape of Things to Come

You now possess the tools to read the hidden architecture of any continuous function. The raw equation gives you coordinates. The first derivative $f'(x)$ gives you velocity and direction. The second derivative $f''(x)$ gives you the structural tension and concavity.

🎓 Key Takeaways from Lecture 14

  • The First Derivative Test: A stationary point ($f'(x)=0$) is a maximum if the slope changes from positive to negative, and a minimum if it changes from negative to positive.
  • Concavity: If $f''(x) > 0$, the curve bends upward like a bowl. If $f''(x) < 0$, it bends downward like an umbrella. Points of inflection occur where $f''(x) = 0$ and the bending reverses.
  • The Second Derivative Test: At a stationary point $x=c$, a positive $f''(c)$ guarantees a local minimum (bottom of the bowl), and a negative $f''(c)$ guarantees a local maximum (top of the umbrella).
