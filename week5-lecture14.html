Week 5, Lecture 14: Monotone Functions & Concavity

Welcome to Lecture 14: Reading the Telemetry of a Curve

In Lecture 13, you mastered the mechanical rules of differentiation. You can now take a raw mathematical equation and instantly generate a new formula representing its rate of change. But what does that new formula actually tell us about the original physical system?

Today, we step back from the algebra and return to the geometry. By analyzing the signs (positive or negative) of a function's first and second derivatives, you can perfectly reconstruct the physical shape of its graph without ever plotting points. The derivatives act as a continuous telemetry stream, telling us exactly where the curve is climbing, falling, peaking, or bending.

🗺️ The Three Layers of Telemetry
  • The Position $f(x)$: Tells us exactly where the system is at a given moment.
  • The Velocity $f'(x)$: Tells us if the system is increasing (moving up) or decreasing (moving down).
  • The Acceleration $f''(x)$: Tells us if the system is bending upward (concave up) or bending downward (concave down).

1. The First Derivative: Tracking Increase & Decrease

If the derivative $f'(x)$ represents the slope of the tangent line, its sign is incredibly informative. A positive slope means the curve is aiming upward; a negative slope means it is aiming downward.

Theorem 14: Monotonicity

Suppose the function $f$ is continuous on $[a, b]$ and differentiable on $(a, b)$.

  • If $f'(x) > 0$ for all $x \in (a, b)$, then $f$ is strictly increasing on $[a, b]$.
  • If $f'(x) < 0$ for all $x \in (a, b)$, then $f$ is strictly decreasing on $[a, b]$.
  • If $f'(x) = 0$ for all $x \in (a, b)$, then $f$ is perfectly constant on $[a, b]$.

To prove this rigorously, mathematicians rely on a foundational concept called the Mean Value Theorem (MVT). Geometrically, the MVT states that if you draw a secant line connecting two points on a smooth curve, there must exist at least one point $c$ strictly between them where the instantaneous tangent is perfectly parallel to the secant line.

Geometric Intuition

The Mean Value Theorem

If your average speed on a road trip was 100 km/h, the MVT guarantees that your speedometer must have struck exactly 100 km/h at least once during the journey. The secant line (average rate of change) guarantees the existence of a parallel tangent line (instantaneous rate of change).

2. Critical Points & The First Derivative Test

If the derivative is positive when the curve is rising, and negative when the curve is falling, what happens at the exact peak of a mountain or the very bottom of a valley? The tangent line must perfectly flatten out. Its slope becomes exactly zero.

Stationary vs. Critical Points

  • Stationary Points: Any point where the derivative equals zero ($f'(x) = 0$). The tangent is completely horizontal.
  • Critical Points: A broader category that includes all stationary points, PLUS any points where the derivative is undefined (such as sharp, jagged corners like the absolute value function $f(x) = |x|$ at $x = 0$).

Theorem 18: If a function $f$ has a relative extremum (a local peak or valley) at $x = c$, then $x = c$ MUST be a critical point. However, the reverse is not always true. Sometimes a curve flattens out to a zero slope but then keeps going up (like a terrace on a mountain). To verify if a critical point is truly a peak or valley, we use the First Derivative Test.

📈 Theorem 19: The First Derivative Test

Let $f$ be continuous at a critical point $x = c$, and differentiable in the surrounding neighborhood. Check the sign of the derivative just before and just after $c$:

  • If $f'(x)$ changes from positive to negative across $c$, the curve rose to a peak and fell. It is a Relative Maximum.
  • If $f'(x)$ changes from negative to positive across $c$, the curve fell into a valley and rose. It is a Relative Minimum.
  • If $f'(x)$ does not change sign (e.g., positive to positive), the point is merely a flat terrace, not an extremum.

3. The Second Derivative: Bending and Concavity

Velocity (the first derivative) tells us if we are moving up or down. But what if we track the rate of change of the rate of change? Taking the derivative of the derivative yields the Second Derivative, denoted $f''(x)$ or $\frac{d^2f}{dx^2}$.

In physics, this is acceleration. In geometry, this tracks the structural bending of the curve, known as concavity.

Concave Up ($f''(x) > 0$)

The slopes are strictly increasing. The curve bends upward like a bowl holding water. Any secant line connecting two points on this section will physically lie above the graph.

Concave Down ($f''(x) < 0$)

The slopes are strictly decreasing. The curve bends downward like an umbrella. Any secant line connecting two points on this section will physically lie below the graph.

🔀 Points of Inflection

What happens at the exact boundary where a curve transitions from being a bowl (concave up) to an umbrella (concave down)? The structural bending briefly zeroes out. If the function has a continuous second derivative across this boundary, it must be that $f''(x) = 0$. These transitional boundaries are called Points of Inflection.

4. The Second Derivative Test

Because the second derivative maps out the "bowls" and "umbrellas" of a function, it gives us a lightning-fast alternative for classifying stationary points without having to check points on either side.

Theorem 20: The Second Derivative Test

Assume $f$ is twice differentiable at a stationary point $x = c$ (meaning $f'(c) = 0$).

  • If $f''(c) > 0$, the curve is a bowl bending upward. The flat spot at $c$ must be the bottom of the bowl: a Relative Minimum.
  • If $f''(c) < 0$, the curve is an umbrella bending downward. The flat spot at $c$ must be the top of the umbrella: a Relative Maximum.
  • If $f''(c) = 0$, the test is inconclusive. You must revert to the First Derivative Test to manually check the slopes on either side.

The Shape of Things to Come

You now possess the tools to read the hidden architecture of any continuous function. The raw equation gives you coordinates. The first derivative $f'(x)$ gives you velocity and direction. The second derivative $f''(x)$ gives you the structural tension and concavity.

🎓 Key Takeaways from Lecture 14

  • The First Derivative Test: A stationary point ($f'(x)=0$) is a maximum if the slope changes from positive to negative, and a minimum if it changes from negative to positive.
  • Concavity: If $f''(x) > 0$, the curve bends upward like a bowl. If $f''(x) < 0$, it bends downward like an umbrella. Points of inflection occur where $f''(x) = 0$ and the bending reverses.
  • The Second Derivative Test: At a stationary point $x=c$, a positive $f''(c)$ guarantees a local minimum (bottom of the bowl), and a negative $f''(c)$ guarantees a local maximum (top of the umbrella).
