1. Sets and Functions

📖 Notation Reference: Sets & Functions

Don't worry if this feels abstract at first: Set theory is simply the friendly art of grouping things together, and functions are just reliable rules that match an input to an output.
$x \in A$$x$ is an element of set $A$
$A \subseteq B$$A$ is a subset of $B$
$A \cup B$Union of sets $A$ and $B$
$A \cap B$Intersection of sets $A$ and $B$
$A \setminus B$Set difference (relative complement)
($A \times B$)Cartesian product of sets
$f: X \to Y$Function $f$ with domain $X$ and codomain $Y$
$f^{-1}(y)$Preimage of element $y$
$g \circ f$Function composition ("f followed by g")
$f^{-1}$Inverse function (exists iff $f$ is bijective)

A set is a collection of distinct elements. New sets are formed via union ($A \cup B$), intersection ($A \cap B$), difference ($A \setminus B$), and Cartesian product ($A \times B$).

Functions and Mappings

A function $f: X \to Y$ assigns to each element $x \in X$ (domain) one and only one value $y = f(x) \in Y$ (codomain).

  • Range: The subset of the codomain consisting of actual output values $R = \{f(x) \mid x \in X\}$.
  • Surjective (Onto): Range equals codomain ($R = Y$), meaning every element in $Y$ has at least one preimage.
  • Injective (1-to-1): Distinct inputs produce distinct outputs: $a \neq b \implies f(a) \neq f(b)$.
  • Bijective: Both injective and surjective, which is the exact necessary and sufficient condition for an inverse function $f^{-1}: Y \to X$ to exist.
  • Composition: For $f: X \to Y$ and $g: Y \to Z$, the composition $g \circ f: X \to Z$ maps $x \mapsto g(f(x))$.

2. Number Systems and Completeness

📖 Notation Reference: Numbers & Bounds

Taking it one step at a time: Every time numbers felt complete, mathematics found a new gap—from counting on our fingers to fractions, and finally to the seamless real number line.
$\mathbb{N}$Natural numbers $\{0, 1, 2, \dots\}$
$\mathbb{Z}$Integers $\{\dots, -1, 0, 1, \dots\}$
$\mathbb{Q}$Rational numbers $\{p/q \mid p \in \mathbb{Z}, q \in \mathbb{Z}_+\}$
$\mathbb{R}$Real numbers (complete ordered field)
$|x|$Absolute value (distance to origin)
$\sup S$Supremum (least upper bound) of set $S$
$\inf S$Infimum (greatest lower bound) of set $S$

Peano's Axioms for $\mathbb{N}$

Natural numbers are defined by Peano's axioms: $0 \in \mathbb{N}$, each number has a unique successor, $0$ is not a successor of any number (no loops, no branching, connected graph rooted at 0), and mathematical induction holds.

Completeness and the Least Upper Bound Property

While rationals $\mathbb{Q}$ are dense, they contain gaps (e.g., $x^2 = 2$ has no rational solution). The real numbers $\mathbb{R}$ extend $\mathbb{Q}$ and satisfy the Axiom of Completeness: Any non-empty subset $S \subseteq \mathbb{R}$ that is bounded above has a supremum ($\sup S$) in $\mathbb{R}$. The Archimedean Axiom ensures that for any positive real numbers $x, y$, there is an $n \in \mathbb{N}$ such that $nx > y$.

3. Sequences

📖 Notation Reference: Sequence Mechanics

Think of a sequence simply as an endless ordered list —like a musical playlist or numbered parking spots—where every step has its own designated number.
$(a_n)$Sequence $(a_0, a_1, a_2, \dots)$
$an + b$Arithmetic progression
$aq^n$Geometric progression

A sequence is an ordered list of numbers mapping indices from $\mathbb{N}$ to $\mathbb{R}$. We can define them explicitly with a closed-form rule or recursively relative to previous terms.

💡 Recipe Analogy: Explicit vs. Recursive Formulas

  • Explicit Formula (Instant Recipe): Tells you exactly how to bake the 100th cake right now without baking the first 99 (e.g., $a_n = 3n + 2$).
  • Recursive Formula (Step-by-Step Recipe): Tells you, "Take yesterday's cake and add two extra strawberries to it." You must know the previous term to find the next one (e.g., $a_1 = 5, a_n = a_{n-1} + 2$).

4. Sums and Partial Sums

📖 Notation Reference: Summation Mechanics

Meet the sigma machine: The Greek letter sigma ($\sum$) is simply shorthand for adding up a string of numbers without having to write out endless plus signs.
$\sum$Sigma operator: shorthand instruction to add numbers together
$\nu = 0$ or $1$Lower limit: the starting index value
$n$Upper limit: the final index value where the sum stops
$b_\nu$General term being added at each step
$s_n$Partial sum: the running total up to index $n$

When studying sequences, we often want to know what happens when we add their terms together. Writing out $b_0 + b_1 + b_2 + \dots + b_n$ gets messy very quickly, so we use summation notation:

$$\sum_{\nu=0}^{n} b_\nu = b_0 + b_1 + b_2 + \dots + b_n$$

Anatomy of a Summation

Think of $\sum$ as a loop in computer programming or an assembly line:

  • The Index ($\nu$): The counter variable that ticks upward by whole numbers.
  • The Starting Point (Lower Limit): Where the counter begins (e.g., $\nu = 0$ or $\nu = 1$).
  • The Stopping Point (Upper Limit): The final number $n$ where the counting finishes.
  • The Formula ($b_\nu$): The rule evaluated at each step of the counter.

Partial Sums: The Running Total

Imagine collecting coins in a video game. Level 0 gives 2 coins, Level 1 gives 3, Level 2 gives 5. Your partial sum ($s_n$) is your running total score up to that exact moment:

$$s_n = \sum_{\nu=0}^{n} b_\nu$$

Two famous summation formulas appear frequently in your coursework:

  • Sum of the First $n$ Integers (Triangular Numbers):
    $$\sum_{\nu=1}^{n} \nu = 1 + 2 + 3 + \dots + n = \frac{n(n+1)}{2}$$
  • Geometric Series Partial Sum:
    $$\sum_{\nu=0}^{n-1} q^\nu = 1 + q + q^2 + \dots + q^{n-1} = \frac{1 - q^n}{1 - q} \quad (\text{for } q \neq 1)$$

⚖️ Side-by-Side Comparison: Derived Sequences vs. Partial Sums

It is very common to mix these two up because both involve mathematical operations on sequences. Here is how to keep them straight:

  • Derived Sequences ($a_n'$): Look locally at immediate neighbors to measure change / speed / slope ($a_{n+1} - a_n$).
  • Partial Sums ($s_n$): Look cumulatively backward at everything that came before to measure total accumulation / area ($\sum b_\nu$).

5. Derived Sequences

📖 Notation Reference: Derived Sequences

The Speedometer Analogy: While a regular sequence tells you your position (like a car odometer), a derived sequence tells you how fast you are jumping from step to step (like a speedometer).
$a_n'$Derived sequence: consecutive differences $a_{n+1} - a_n$

When you look at a regular sequence, you see its position at each step. A derived sequence ($a_n' = a_{n+1} - a_n$) answers a single question: "How much did the sequence jump from one step to the next?"

$$a_n' = a_{n+1} - a_n$$

Worked Example: Squares and Differences

Consider the sequence of squares $(a_n) = (1, 4, 9, 16, 25, \dots)$, where $a_n = n^2$. Let's calculate its derived sequence step by step:

  • $a_1' = a_2 - a_1 = 4 - 1 = 3$
  • $a_2' = a_3 - a_2 = 9 - 4 = 5$
  • $a_3' = a_4 - a_3 = 16 - 9 = 7$
  • $a_4' = a_5 - a_4 = 25 - 16 = 9$

The resulting derived sequence is $(3, 5, 7, 9, \dots)$, which follows the explicit formula $a_n' = 2n + 1$.

Connecting Derived Sequences to Behavior

Derived sequences give us an instant test for monotonicity:

  • Increasing: If $a_n' \ge 0$ for all $n$, the sequence never steps backward, so it is monotonically increasing.
  • Decreasing: If $a_n' \le 0$ for all $n$, the sequence is monotonically decreasing.
  • Constant: If $a_n' = 0$ for all $n$, every term is identical.
