Week 6 Overview Hub

Welcome to Week 6: The Accumulation of Change

If differentiation (Week 5) is about dissecting a process to find its instantaneous rate of change, integration is about putting the pieces back together. How do we take a variable rate of change—like a fluctuating speed—and reconstruct the total distance travelled?

This week, we learn to compute the accumulation of quantities by finding the area under a curve. We will build this concept from the ground up using microscopic rectangles, before unlocking one of the most profound discoveries in human history: the theorem that proves differentiation and integration are perfect mathematical inverses.

The Big Picture: Slicing, Summing, and Solving

In Lecture 16 (Integration), we tackle the problem of measuring non-standard shapes. We sandwich the area under a curve between upper and lower staircases (Darboux sums). By taking the limit as the number of slices approaches infinity, we capture the exact area, officially defining the Definite Riemann Integral.

Lecture 17 (Properties of the Definite Integral) establishes the physical laws of this new tool. We learn how to split areas into smaller adjacent regions, apply linearity, and use the Mean Value Theorem of Integral Calculus to find the exact "average height" of a fluctuating wave.

Finally, in Lecture 18 (The Fundamental Theorem of Calculus), we bypass the exhausting process of summing infinite rectangles. We discover that to find the exact area under a curve, we simply need to find the function's antiderivative (primitive) and evaluate its boundaries. This theorem beautifully bridges Week 5 and Week 6.
